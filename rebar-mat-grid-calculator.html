How to use: Rebar Mat & Grid Calculator

  1. Enter mat length and width.
  2. Set the grid spacing and edge offset.
  3. Pick a bar size for the totals.
  4. Read the results: bar count in each direction, tie intersections, total bar length and weight.
  5. Order extra for laps between mats, tie wire and cutting waste — the calculator gives the neat mat weight only (see the tie wire calculator).

Formula

Bars each way = floor((dimension − 2 × offset) ÷ spacing) + 1. Intersections = nx × ny.

See the full rebar weight chart (kg/m & lb/ft) →

Why minus twice the offset? The outer bars sit inside the mat edge by the offset on both sides, so the offset is removed from each end before counting. Why plus one? The first bar sits at the offset line and then one bar follows every spacing step — so the count is the number of spaces plus one, not just the number of spaces. Why both directions? A mat is a two-way grid, and the second direction usually adds about as much steel again.

Bar weight per metre comes from the standard formula kg/m = d² ÷ 162.28, where d is the bar diameter in mm. For a 10 mm bar: 10² ÷ 162.28 = 0.616 kg/m — the same value the calculator uses.

The orange dashed line marks where the bars actually run: inside the offset, on both sides. Bars cross in both directions, and every crossing is one tie intersection.

Worked example 1: 6 m × 3 m mat

Mat 6 m × 3 m, 10 mm bars (0.616 kg/m), 200 mm spacing, 25 mm edge offset. These are the values the calculator opens with, so you can check the result immediately:

  1. Bars along the 6 m direction: floor((3,000 − 50) ÷ 200) + 1 = 15 bars, each 5.95 m long = 89.25 m.
  2. Bars along the 3 m direction: floor((6,000 − 50) ÷ 200) + 1 = 30 bars, each 2.95 m long = 88.50 m.
  3. Total bar length: 89.25 + 88.50 = 177.75 m.
  4. Total weight: 177.75 × 0.616 = 109.49 kg.
  5. Tie intersections: 15 × 30 = 450.

Try it yourself: enter the same numbers in the calculator above and you will get the same result.

Worked example 2: 4 m × 4 m mat

Mat 4 m × 4 m, 12 mm bars (0.887 kg/m), 150 mm spacing, 25 mm edge offset:

  1. Bars along one 4 m direction: floor((4,000 − 50) ÷ 150) + 1 = 27 bars, each 3.95 m long = 106.65 m.
  2. Bars along the other 4 m direction: the same = 27 bars, each 3.95 m long = 106.65 m.
  3. Total bar length: 106.65 + 106.65 = 213.30 m.
  4. Total weight: 213.30 × 0.887 = 189.20 kg.
  5. Tie intersections: 27 × 27 = 729.

Notice how the square mat needs the same count both ways, but the tighter 150 mm spacing pushes the tie count to 729 — every extra bar adds a full row of ties.

Bars per metre = 1,000 ÷ spacing in mm. Halving the spacing roughly doubles the steel. Mats commonly use 150–200 mm centres — but spacing is a design decision from your structural drawings, never a guess from a table. If your drawing says 200 mm, use 200 mm even if 300 mm would be cheaper.

Estimating the cost

Cost = total weight × price per kg (or total tonnes × price per tonne). Using worked example 1: 109.49 kg × €1.20/kg = €131.39 per mat. Five identical mats: 547.47 kg × €1.20/kg = €656.96. Prices vary by region, supplier and bar size, so get a current quote before budgeting — and remember the order quantity is higher than the neat mat weight once laps between mats, tie wire and waste are added. What the calculator does not include

  • Tie wire. The calculator counts intersections (450 in example 1) but not the wire itself. Estimate wire from intersections with the tie wire calculator.
  • Laps between mats. Where one mat meets the next, bars must overlap. Find the overlap with the lap length calculator and add it to the length.
  • Chairs and spacers. The mat needs support to stay at the right height and cover. Estimate those with the chair calculator.
  • Cutting waste. Cutting bars to length always leaves offcuts. Add a waste allowance on top of the neat weight before ordering.
  • Hooks and bends. Edge hooks and bent-up bars add length. Use the hook and cutting length calculator for those.
  • Welded mesh sheets. If you buy prefabricated mesh instead of loose bars, the sheet sizes and weights are fixed — this calculator covers loose-bar mats.

4 common mistakes

  1. Confusing offset with cover. The edge offset is the distance from the mat edge to the first bar. Concrete cover — the concrete over the mat — is a separate value set by the design and the supports, not by this calculator.
  2. Counting one direction only. A mat runs both ways. The second direction usually adds about as much steel again.
  3. Mixing up mm and m. Spacing and offset are in millimetres, mat size in metres. Entering 200 as metres instead of millimetres breaks the result completely.
  4. Ordering exactly the calculated weight. Add laps between mats, tie wire and cutting waste on top — the calculator gives the neat mat weight, not the order quantity.

Rebar Mat & Grid Calculator — FAQ

What is a rebar mat?

A grid of bars tied together in two directions, used in slabs, rafts and footings. Mats can be tied on site or delivered prefabricated; either way, the bar count, length and weight come from the same two-way grid maths.

How many tie points are in a mat?

One at each bar intersection — the calculator reports this as the intersection count. The 6 m × 3 m mat in example 1 has 15 × 30 = 450 intersections, so 450 ties if every crossing is tied.

How do I calculate a rebar mat by hand?

Each way: bars = floor((dimension − 2 × offset) ÷ spacing) + 1, and each bar is (dimension − 2 × offset) long. Add both directions for total length, then multiply by kg/m for weight. Worked example 1 above shows every step.

What is the edge offset?

The distance from the edge of the mat to the first bar — 25 mm by default here. It is not the concrete cover: cover is the concrete over the finished mat, set by the design and the chairs or spacers that hold the mat up.

What bar size and spacing are used for mats?

10–12 mm bars at 150–200 mm centres are typical for slab and raft mats; heavier mats use 16 mm or more. Size and spacing come from the structural design — this calculator sizes the quantity once you have them.

How much does a 6 m × 3 m mat weigh?

At 200 mm centres with 10 mm bars and a 25 mm offset: 15 bars one way and 30 the other (45 bars), 177.75 m of bar, 109.49 kg, and 450 tie intersections. Change the spacing or bar size in the calculator to see how fast the weight moves.

How much tie wire do I need for a mat?

It depends on the intersection count and the wire used per tie. Take the intersection count from this calculator — 450 for the 6 m × 3 m mat — and enter it in the tie wire calculator for the wire length and weight.

How much does a rebar mat cost?

Multiply the weight by your local price per kg. At €1.20/kg, the 6 m × 3 m mat in example 1 costs €131.39 in steel, and five identical mats cost €656.96. That is the neat mat weight only — laps, tie wire and waste add to the order.

Can I use this calculator for welded mesh sheets?

For estimating, yes — the grid maths is the same. But mesh sheets come in fixed sizes with fixed wire diameters, so the exact sheet count and weight come from the manufacturer's sheet table, not from loose-bar lengths.

What is the difference between a mat and a slab grid?

There is no maths difference — a mat is the reinforcement itself, while a slab grid is the same two-way layout described as part of a slab. The calculator works for both; what changes is only what you call it and how it is placed.
