How to use: Rebar Cost Calculator

  1. Get the total neat weight first — from the weight calculator, the tonnage calculator, or a takeoff on the quantity calculator.
  2. Enter the total weight in kilograms.
  3. Enter your supplier's price per kg — divide a per-tonne quote by 1000 first.
  4. Set the wastage percentage (the default is 3%; use 5–10% for bent or complex work).
  5. Read the estimated cost, the weight including wastage, the cost per ton and the tonnage — then add tax, delivery and labour separately, since the panel prices material only.

Formula

Cost = weight × (1 + wastage%) × price per kg.

The formula, explained

The panel does two things in order: first it adds waste to the weight, then it prices the result. That order matters, because you pay for the wasted steel too:

Weight with waste = weight × (1 + wastage ÷ 100); Cost = weight with waste × price per kg; Cost per ton = price per kg × 1000

On the defaults: 1000 × 1.03 = 1,030 kg with waste, and 1,030 × 0.85 = 875.50. The panel shows four rows — estimated cost 875.50, weight + wastage 1,030 kg, cost per ton 850.00, in tons 1.03 — with the note "1,030 kg × 0.85/kg (incl. 3% waste)". Three display notes. First, the cost row carries NO currency symbol: enter the rate in your own currency and the result is in that currency — unlike the USD-only tools elsewhere, this panel never assumes dollars. Second, totals at or above 1000 show one decimal with separators ("1,030", "3,150"), totals below 1000 show two decimals ("875.50", "309.00"), and tonnages below 1 show three decimals ("0.515", "0.054") — quote the panel forms and your reader's check will match. Third, the default rate of 0.85 per kg is a placeholder, not a market price: steel rates move with the market, and every rate in this page is labelled an example rate.

Worked example 1: defaults (1000 kg)

Open the calculator as it loads: weight 1000 kg, price 0.85 per kg, wastage 3%.

Weight with waste = 1000 × 1.03 = 1,030 kg. Cost = 1,030 × 0.85 = 875.50. Cost per ton = 0.85 × 1000 = 850.00. Tons = 1,030 ÷ 1000 = 1.03.

Estimated cost 875.50, for 1,030 kg (1.03 t) at 850.00 per ton. The panel shows all four rows with the note "1,030 kg × 0.85/kg (incl. 3% waste)". Two readings. The cost per ton (850.00) is the rate converted, not the cost divided by neat tonnes — the reading-the-panel section explains why that distinction matters. And the waste costs real money here: at 0% waste the same tonne costs 850.00, so the 3% allowance adds 25.50. Waste is never free — it is steel you buy and cut away.

Worked example 2: 2500 kg order

Set weight 2500 kg, price 1.20 per kg, wastage 5%.

Weight with waste = 2500 × 1.05 = 2,625 kg. Cost = 2,625 × 1.20 = 3,150. Cost per ton = 1.20 × 1000 = 1,200. Tons = 2,625 ÷ 1000 = 2.63 (exact 2.625, displayed to two decimals).

Estimated cost 3,150, for 2,625 kg (2.63 t) at 1,200 per ton. Note the panel form: the cost is "3,150" (one decimal rule for totals at or above 1000 — an integer total shows no decimal), and the tonnage is "2.63", not 2.625. If you re-run this order and see 2.63 t, that is the panel rounding, not a different answer. At 3% waste instead of 5%, the same order would be 2,575 kg and 3,090 — the two extra waste points cost 60.00 at this rate, which is why the waste percentage deserves a decision rather than a shrug.

Worked example 3: small 500 kg order

Set weight 500 kg, price 0.60 per kg, wastage 3%.

Weight with waste = 500 × 1.03 = 515.00 kg. Cost = 515.00 × 0.60 = 309.00. Cost per ton = 0.60 × 1000 = 600.00. Tons = 515.00 ÷ 1000 = 0.515.

Estimated cost 309.00, for 515.00 kg (0.515 t) at 600.00 per ton. Small orders show the panel's other display rule: below 1000, two decimals ("309.00", "515.00"), and below 1 tonne, three decimals ("0.515"). Raise the waste to 10% and the same order becomes 550.00 kg, 330.00, 0.550 t — the 7 extra waste points cost 21.00. On small orders the waste percentage moves the total by tens, not hundreds, but the percentage error is identical — and on a small order a supplier's minimum-order or delivery charge (both outside this panel) can exceed the waste cost entirely.

Reading the panel: per-ton and rounding

Two distinctions that silently corrupt quote comparisons when they are missed.

Per tonne bought vs per tonne needed. The panel's "Cost per ton" is rate × 1000 — the price per tonne of steel you order, waste included. On the defaults: 875.50 ÷ 1.03 t = 850.00. But the cost per tonne of neat steel in the structure is 875.50 ÷ 1.00 = 875.50, because the 30 kg of waste is bought and paid for. If a supplier quotes "850 per tonne" and your takeoff is neat, your true material cost per neat tonne is 850 × 1.03 = 875.50 at 3% waste — the quote and the cost differ by exactly the waste percentage. Always ask which tonne a per-tonne quote means.

Panel rounding vs exact products. Totals at or above 1000 display with one decimal, so the cost panel shows "1,341.1" where the exact product is 1,341.144, and "1,405" where it is 1,405.008 (see the bridges section). The weight page's order bridge shows the exact 2-decimal products (€1,341.14 and €1,405.01) for the same orders. Both are right; the panel rounds for display. Below 1000 the panel shows two decimals, so the quantity page's €388.08 and €406.56 match this panel exactly. A last-decimal difference between two pages is a rounding difference until proven otherwise.

What a budget buys (reverse)

The panel runs weight → cost only. The reverse direction — what a fixed budget buys — is simple arithmetic, labelled here as derived (not a panel output): neat weight = budget ÷ ((1 + waste) × rate).

Budget (example rate 1.20/kg, 5% waste)Neat steel boughtOrdered weight (with waste)
1000793.65 kg833.33 kg
50003,968.25 kg4,166.67 kg
100007,936.51 kg8,333.33 kg

Worked: 1000 ÷ (1.05 × 1.20) = 1000 ÷ 1.26 = 793.65 kg neat; the ordered weight is 793.65 × 1.05 = 833.33 kg. The waste eats the budget twice over in a sense: it raises the ordered weight AND it is steel you pay for but do not keep — on a 10000 budget at 5% waste, 396.83 kg of the order (8,333.33 − 7,936.51) is waste steel. If the budget is tight, the waste percentage is the first lever to challenge — but only by improving the cutting plan, never by deleting the allowance and hoping.

Wastage guidance by structure

The panel's default is 3%, and the live guidance has always been 3–5%. Published structure-by-structure guidance refines it — use the row that matches the work, not the default, when the work is complex:

StructureSuggested wastageWhy
Columns3–5%Mostly straight verticals with ties
Footings5–7%Heavy bars, lap splices
Slabs5–7%Grid pattern, two-way cutting
Walls5–7%Horizontal + vertical bars
Beams5–8%Stirrups included in the count
Stairs8–10%Complex shapes, multiple bends

Two rules sit above the table. First, laps are not waste: a lap is steel in the structure, and if your takeoff already includes laps (from the lap calculator) and hooks (from the hook calculator), the waste percentage covers cutting and handling only (3–5%). If your takeoff is neat straight lengths, use a combined allowance — published takeoff guides use 10% for laps and waste together — or add the laps explicitly. Applying 10% on top of explicit laps pays for the laps twice. Second, the cutting plan beats the percentage: a schedule of 3 m cuts from 12 m bars wastes almost nothing, while 5 m cuts leave 2 m offcuts everywhere — no percentage fixes a bad cutting plan, and no cutting plan needs a percentage it cannot justify.

Imperial and per-tonne conversions

The panel takes kilograms and a per-kg rate. Convert first, then enter — the panel never converts for you.

Rate conversions (derived arithmetic). Per metric tonne = per-kg rate × 1000 (0.85 → 850; 1.20 → 1,200). Per US short ton = per-kg rate × 907.185 (0.85 → 771.11; 1.20 → 1,088.62). Per pound = per-kg rate ÷ 2.20462 (0.85 → 0.386/lb; 1.20 → 0.544/lb). In the other direction: per-kg = per-tonne ÷ 1000; per-kg = per-short-ton ÷ 907.185; per-kg = per-lb × 2.20462 (0.55/lb → 1.21254/kg).

Worked imperial example. An order of 2000 lb at $0.55 per lb with 5% waste: weight with waste = 2000 × 1.05 = 2100 lb; cost = 2100 × 0.55 = $1,155.00. In metric terms the same order is 907.18 kg neat (2000 × 0.453592) and 952.54 kg ordered. The per-tonne equivalents of $0.55/lb are $1,100.00 per short ton (0.55 × 2000) and $1,212.54 per metric tonne (0.55 × 2204.62) — the $112.54 gap between those two "per ton" figures is purely the ton definition, and it is the single most common cause of a quote looking 10% cheaper than it is. To enter this order in the panel, use 907.18 kg at 1.21 per kg (the rounded form of 1.21254); the panel result will differ from $1,155.00 by the rate rounding (about $2.40), so for exact imperial pricing, price in lb/$ as above rather than through a rounded per-kg rate.

What this calculator does not cover

  • Tax / VAT — add your local rate to the panel total by hand.
  • Labour: cutting, bending, placing, tying — price by hours × rate or by a per-kg/per-ton labour rate from local benchmarks.
  • Cutting and bending charges, binding wire, chairs, spacers and cover blocks — see the chair calculator and the tie wire calculator for the accessory quantities.
  • Delivery and transport, unloading, and crane/hoist time.
  • Concrete, formwork and other materials — concrete is on the concrete estimator.
  • Per-size or per-grade pricing — the panel takes one rate for all steel; price diameters separately if your supplier does.
  • Laps and hooks as separate lines — the waste percentage is the only allowance; work laps on the lap calculator and hooks on the hook calculator and add them to the neat weight first if you want them explicit.
  • Bar counts, lengths and schedules — the weight comes from the weight calculator, the tonnage calculator or a member tool; this panel only prices a weight you give it.
  • Stainless or alloy densities, mesh, and cut-length optimisation against stock bars.
  • Price validity — the panel stores no market data; a rate entered today is not a price tomorrow.
  • Currency conversion — the panel shows no currency symbol; convert the rate yourself before entering it.

5 common mistakes

  1. Pricing the neat weight. Entering the takeoff weight with 0% waste prices steel you will cut away as if it were free. On the defaults, 0% waste gives 850.00 and 3% gives 875.50 — the 25.50 difference is the cheapest insurance on the order. Even a perfect cutting plan loses bars to handling damage.
  2. Paying for laps twice. Adding a 10% combined laps-and-waste allowance on top of a takeoff that already includes explicit laps (from the lap calculator) over-orders by the full lap weight — on a 10-tonne order, a tonne of phantom steel. Use combined OR explicit, never both.
  3. Entering a per-tonne rate into the per-kg field. The panel's rate field is per kg. Entering 850 (a per-tonne rate) prices the defaults order at 875,500 instead of 875.50 — a 1000× error that survives a casual glance because the panel shows no currency unit to contradict it. Divide per-tonne quotes by 1000 first; the cost-per-ton row is the check (it should read close to the quoted per-tonne rate).
  4. Comparing per-tonne quotes across ton definitions. A quote of 771.11 per US short ton and a quote of 850 per metric tonne are the SAME rate (0.85/kg) — the short ton is 907.185 kg, not 1000 kg. A short-ton quote looks ~10% cheaper than the same rate per metric tonne, and the gap has fooled more than one budget. Convert every quote to per-kg before comparing.
  5. Trusting a stale rate, or a competitor's worked example, without re-running it. Steel prices move, and published examples do not always reproduce: one popular combined tool's slab example claims ~71 kg where its own stated formula gives 74.71 kg, and one popular guide's imperial formula overstates #8 bar by 16× (42.75 lb/ft vs the true 2.670 lb/ft). Re-run every example in the panel with your own rate before relying on it — including the ones on this page.

Rebar Cost Calculator — FAQ

How much does rebar cost?

It varies by market and size; enter your local price per kg for an accurate estimate. The panel itself quotes no market price — its default rate of 0.85 per kg is only a placeholder, and the rate table below shows what the same tonne costs at 0.50, 0.75, 0.85, 1.00, 1.20, 1.50 and 2.00 per kg. Always get a current supplier quote: one published estimating guide lists a typical input range of 0.5–3.0 per kg, which is a range, not a price. And check the quote's basis — per kg, per metric tonne, or per US short ton — before comparing (see the conversions section).

What wastage should I allow for rebar?

Typically 3–5% to cover offcuts and laps — that is the panel's default of 3% and the live guidance it has always carried. Refine it by structure, from published guidance: columns 3–5%, footings 5–7%, slabs 5–7%, walls 5–7%, beams 5–8%, stairs 8–10% (complex shapes, multiple bends). Straight bars need the least; bent bars, hooks and tight cutting plans need the most. One caution: if your takeoff already includes laps (from the lap calculator), the waste percentage should cover cutting and handling only — adding a combined 10% on top of explicit laps double-counts. The waste section below works both cases.

Does the cost include labour, delivery or tax?

No — the panel prices material only: weight × (1 + waste) × rate. Everything else sits outside it: cutting and bending charges, binding wire and accessories (chairs, spacers, cover blocks — see the chair calculator and the tie wire calculator), delivery and transport, and tax/VAT. Real rate build-ups separate these lines — one published analysis sheet, for example, builds a per-kg rate from material + labour + 15% overhead and profit — so a material-only figure must never be presented as an installed price. Add the other lines by hand after the panel.

What does "Cost per ton" mean on the panel?

It is your rate × 1000 — the price per tonne of steel you BUY, including the waste allowance, not per tonne you NEED. On the defaults (1000 kg, 0.85/kg, 3%) it reads 850.00, and indeed 875.50 ÷ 1.03 t = 850.00. But the cost per tonne of neat steel is 875.50 ÷ 1.00 = 875.50, because you pay for the 30 kg of waste too. When a supplier quotes a per-tonne rate, ask whether it is per tonne ordered or per tonne in the structure — the two differ by exactly the waste percentage.

Why does the panel show 1,341.1 when my multiplication gives 1,341.14?

Because the panel rounds totals at or above 1000 to one decimal: 1064.4 kg × 1.05 × 1.20 = 1,341.144 exactly, which displays as "1,341.1". Same money, panel rounding — and the same applies to the weight page's bridge figures, which show the exact 2-decimal products (€1,341.14 and €1,405.01) for the same orders that read 1,341.1 and 1,405 here. Below 1000 the panel shows two decimals, so the quantity page's €388.08 and €406.56 match this panel exactly. Never treat a last-decimal difference between two pages as an error without checking the rounding first.

How do I convert a price per tonne or per pound to price per kg?

Divide a per-metric-tonne quote by 1000: 850 per tonne = 0.85 per kg; 1,200 per tonne = 1.20 per kg. Divide a per-US-short-ton quote by 907.185: 1,100 per short ton = 1.21254 per kg (enter 1.21). Multiply a per-pound quote by 2.20462: 0.55 per lb = 1.21254 per kg. The conversions section works all three directions with the defaults rate, and the imperial example prices a 2000 lb order end to end.

How much steel can my budget buy?

The panel has no reverse mode, so work it backwards by hand: neat weight = budget ÷ ((1 + waste) × rate). At an example 1.20 per kg with 5% waste, a budget of 1000 buys 793.65 kg of neat steel (1000 ÷ 1.26), 5000 buys 3,968.25 kg, and 10000 buys 7,936.51 kg. (Derived arithmetic, not a panel output — the panel only runs weight → cost.) Remember the result is neat weight: the ordered weight is 5% higher, and the bars still have to fit stock lengths.

Should I add laps on top of the waste percentage?

Only if your takeoff does not already include them. Two consistent ways exist, and mixing them is the error: either take off neat straight lengths and use a combined allowance (published takeoff guides use 10% for laps and waste together), or take off explicit laps from the lap calculator and hooks from the hook calculator and then apply waste of 3–5% for cutting and handling only. Applying 10% on top of explicit laps pays for the laps twice — on a 10-tonne order that is a full tonne of phantom steel.

Why do two suppliers' quotes differ for the same weight?

Usually the basis differs, not the arithmetic. Check, in order: (1) the rate basis — per kg, per metric tonne, or per US short ton (a short-ton quote looks ~10% cheaper than the same rate per metric tonne); (2) whether waste is included in the rate or added separately; (3) theoretical vs actual (weighed) weight — delivered bars vary within tolerance, and the two bases never agree to the kilogram; (4) whether delivery, cutting/bending and tax are in or out; (5) whether one rate covers all diameters or each size prices separately; (6) the quote's date — steel prices move, and an old quote is not a price. Convert every quote to the same basis (per kg, waste stated, extras listed) before comparing.

Can I price a whole project with one rate?

Only as a first estimate. The panel takes a single rate for all steel, but suppliers often price diameters differently, and a project mixes sizes, grades and members. For a defensible figure: take off each member on its own tool (slab, footing, mat, column, beam, wall, pool, driveway), price each weight here at its own rate if rates differ, add the detailing extras (chairs, tie wire, hooks, laps), then add concrete on the concrete estimator and labour, delivery and tax by hand. One rate × one weight is a budget; the member-by-member build is a quote.
