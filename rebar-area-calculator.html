How to use: Rebar Area Calculator

  1. Pick the bar size — metric (mm) or US (# size) — from the dropdown.
  2. Enter the number of bars in the section.
  3. Optionally enter the section width and depth in mm to get the steel percentage. Leave them at the defaults (300 × 450 mm) for a quick check, or set either to 0 if you only want the area — the percentage then reads 0.000%.
  4. Read the total steel area Ast in mm² and cm², the area of one bar, and the steel percentage.
  5. If the section mixes two bar sizes, run the calculator once per size and add the two Ast figures — see the two-size section below. If you need area per metre from a spacing, use the per-metre table below.

Formula

Area of one bar = π/4 × d². Ast = number of bars × area. Steel % = Ast ÷ (b × D) × 100.

The formula, explained

A bar's cross-section is a circle, so its area is π/4 × d², where d is the nominal diameter in mm. That is the whole formula — everything else is multiplication or division. Total steel area Ast = number of bars × one-bar area. Steel percentage = Ast ÷ (section width × section depth) × 100, using the GROSS section (overall depth D), not the effective depth d — the distinction matters for code checks, and the beam example below shows both figures for the same cage. Ast in cm² is simply Ast in mm² ÷ 100, which is why the panel shows 452.39 mm² as 4.52 cm². Two derived forms used on this page: area per metre = one-bar area ÷ spacing in metres, and spacing from a required area per metre = one-bar area × 1000 ÷ required Ast per metre. Note the panel's percentage behaviour: if either section dimension is 0, the gross area is 0 and the panel shows 0.000% — that means "no section entered", not "no steel".

Worked example 1: the calculator defaults

Open the calculator as it loads: 12 mm bars, 4 bars, section 300 × 450 mm. One bar is π/4 × 12² = 113.10 mm². Four bars give Ast = 4 × 113.10 = 452.39 mm² (4.52 cm²). The gross section is 300 × 450 = 135,000 mm², so the steel percentage is 452.39 ÷ 135,000 × 100 = 0.335%. The panel shows all four rows: Ast 452.39 mm², one bar 113.10 mm², steel percentage 0.335%, Ast 4.52 cm², with the note "Ast = 4 × π/4 × 12² = 452.39 mm²". The same 452.39 mm² appears in the competitor BuildingClub's slab example — the formula is universal; what this page adds is everything around it.

Worked example 2: a beam cage against its minimum

Take the beam page's defaults cage: 5 × 16 mm bars in a 230 × 450 mm beam. Enter 16 mm, 5 bars, 230 × 450 mm. One bar is 201.06 mm², so Ast = 1,005.3 mm² (10.05 cm²) and the panel percentage is 1,005.3 ÷ 103,500 × 100 = 0.971% of gross area. Now the code check, which divides differently: the beam minimum is 0.85/fy of b × d, and for Fe415 with effective depth 409 mm that is 192.67 mm² — the cage clears it five times over. The maximum is 4% of gross area, 4,140.00 mm², which it sits well inside. And the percentage against b × d (230 × 409 = 94,070 mm²) is 1.07%, not 0.971% — same bars, different divisor. If a code clause says b × d, recompute against b × d; do not compare the panel's gross percentage directly with it.

Worked example 3: a wall strip that fails its minimum

Per-metre checks run through the same panel by entering a 1000 mm wide strip: the bar count is then the bars per metre. Try 10 mm bars, 5 bars (the count at 200 mm spacing), section 1000 × 200 mm. One bar is 78.54 mm², so Ast = 392.70 mm² (3.93 cm²) and the percentage is 392.70 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = 0.196%. That FAILS the wall horizontal minimum of 0.20% (400 mm²/m on a 200 mm wall) — the same failure the wall page flags for its defaults grid. The fix is one bar size up: 5 × 12 mm gives 565.49 mm² (5.65 cm²), 0.283%, which passes. This is the check worth running before ordering: a grid that looks reasonable can sit just under a minimum, and the panel percentage catches it in seconds.

Equivalent bar combinations

When a design specifies a required area, any combination at or above it works on area (spacing, cover, laps and development still need their own checks). Divide the required area by the one-bar area and round UP — the table shows why rounding down fails: 7 × 12 mm (791.68 mm²) looks close to 804.25 mm² but is 12.57 mm² short, so the passing choice is 8 × 12 mm (904.78 mm²) or the exact 4 × 16 mm.

Going the other way — spacing from a required area per metre — use spacing = one-bar area × 1000 ÷ required Ast per metre. Two worked substitutions: 10 mm bars at 100 mm give 785.40 mm²/m, and 12 mm bars need 113.10 ÷ 785.40 × 1000 = 144.00 mm spacing for the same area (the textbook answer is 14.40 cm — same figure). 12 mm bars at 200 mm give 565.49 mm²/m, and 16 mm bars need 201.06 ÷ 565.49 × 1000 = 355.56 mm spacing for the same area; in practice 350 mm gives 574.46 mm²/m (slightly over) and 355 mm gives 566.37 mm²/m, so round the spacing DOWN to stay at or above the required area. The shortcut for swapping sizes at fixed area is s₂ = (d₂/d₁)² × s₁ — (12/10)² × 100 = 144 mm, matching the long way.

Two bar sizes in one section

Real cages mix sizes: top bars differ from bottom bars, and columns mix corner and face bars. This calculator takes one size per run, so run it once per size and add. A beam with 2 × 20 mm top bars and 3 × 16 mm bottom bars is 628.32 + 603.19 = 1,231.5 mm² (each half verified on the panel separately). A column with 4 × 16 mm plus 4 × 20 mm is 804.25 + 1,256.6 = 2,060.9 mm², which is 1.53% of a 300 × 450 mm gross section — inside the 0.8–6% column bracket. What NOT to add: stirrup/tie steel is not part of Ast, lap steel double-counts bars in the lap zone (use the lap length, not extra area), and compression steel is a separate Ast/Asc figure in design even though the panel adds whatever bars you enter.

Minimum steel checks

Passing a minimum is not a design — it only catches gross errors — but it is the fastest sanity check on any cage, and the panel percentage feeds straight into it. Note which area each clause divides by: beams divide by b × d, the rest below by gross area (per metre of width for slabs and walls).

If a cage fails a minimum, the fix is a bigger area — one size up, or tighter spacing — re-run through the panel and re-check. If it passes, that still says nothing about bending, shear, deflection, laps, hooks or development: those live on the companion pages.

Imperial bars and unit conversions

US bars are sized by eighths of an inch (#4 = 4/8 in = 1/2 in), but from #9 up the diameters follow legacy area-based sizes, so always use the nominal diameter in mm from the table — not the eighths rule — for area. Two traps: a #4 bar (12.7 mm, 126.68 mm²) is NOT a 12 mm bar (113.10 mm²) — swapping them blindly changes the area by 12.0%. And Canadian M-bars are labelled by nominal AREA, not diameter (15M ≈ 200 mm² at 16.0 mm diameter) — a 15M bar is not a 15 mm bar.

What this calculator does not do

This is an area tool, not a design or ordering tool. It does not size reinforcement from loads or moments (required Ast comes from the structural design, e.g. As ≈ M ÷ (0.87 × fy × d) — a design calculation, not a panel output); it does not give weight, length, cost or tonnage; it does not count bars from a spacing layout or a slab size; it does not check laps, hooks, cranks, development or anchorage; it does not include stirrup/tie steel, chairs, or tie wire; and it does not check shear, deflection, crack width, or spacing/cover rules. Use the companion pages for the missing pieces: weight, quantity and takeoff, spacing, development length, lap length, hooks, stirrups, columns, beams, walls, pools, chairs, tie wire, cost and tonnage.

Common mistakes

Swapping #4 for 12 mm. A #4 bar is 12.7 mm, not 12 mm: 126.68 mm² vs 113.10 mm². On a 10-bar cage that is 135.80 mm² of phantom (or missing) steel. Match the diameter, not the nickname.

Comparing the panel percentage with a b × d rule. The panel divides by gross b × D. A beam minimum divides by b × d. The same 5 × 16 mm cage reads 0.971% on the panel and 1.07% against b × d — quoting the wrong one against a code limit gives a false pass or a false fail.

Rounding the bar count down. Required 804.25 mm² ÷ 113.10 = 7.11 bars is 8 bars, not 7. Seven 12 mm bars give 791.68 mm² — 12.57 mm² short — and "close enough" is not a passing section.

Adding rounded areas. Adding published 1-decimal or 2-decimal in² figures drifts: 4 × 0.44 in² = 1.76 in², but the exact area is 1.78 in². Add exact mm² figures (or the panel's unrounded logic), then round once at the end.

Running a mixed cage once. One run takes one size. A 2 × 20 + 3 × 16 cage entered as "5 bars, 16 mm" gives 1,005.3 mm² instead of the true 1,231.5 mm² — 226.20 mm² missing. Run each size separately and add.

Rebar Area Calculator — FAQ

What is the area of a 12mm rebar?

π/4 × 12² = 113.10 mm² per bar (113.1 mm² rounded). Four 12 mm bars give 452.39 mm² — try it on the calculator defaults.

What is Ast in reinforcement?

Ast is the total cross-sectional area of steel in a section: the area of one bar multiplied by the number of bars. On the calculator defaults (4 × 12 mm) Ast is 452.39 mm², or 4.52 cm². It is the area the structural design provides, and it is what minimum-steel checks compare against the concrete section.

How do you calculate the area of steel?

Work out one bar first: area = π/4 × diameter². A 16 mm bar is π/4 × 16² = 201.06 mm². Then multiply by the number of bars: 5 × 16 mm gives 1,005.3 mm². The calculator does both steps and, if you enter the section size, divides by the gross section to give the steel percentage.

What is the steel percentage, and is it against the gross or the effective depth?

This calculator divides Ast by the gross section (width × overall depth). On the defaults that is 452.39 ÷ (300 × 450) × 100 = 0.335%. Beam minimum-steel rules, by contrast, are written against b × d (width × effective depth), so the same cage shows a higher percentage there — 5 × 16 mm in a 230 × 450 mm beam is 0.971% of gross but 1.07% of b × d (230 × 409). Always check which area a code clause divides by before comparing percentages.

How many bars do I need for a required steel area?

Divide the required area by the area of one bar and round UP — you cannot provide part of a bar, and rounding down leaves the section short. For a required 804.25 mm², 4 × 16 mm gives exactly 804.25 mm², while 7 × 12 mm gives only 791.68 mm² (short) and 8 × 12 mm gives 904.78 mm² (the next size up that passes). Can I replace one bar size with another?

Yes, if the total area stays equal or greater — and if spacing, cover and development length still work. The area rule is what matters, not the count: 10 mm bars at 100 mm spacing give 785.40 mm² per metre, and 12 mm bars need 144.00 mm spacing for the same area per metre (113.10 ÷ 785.40 × 1000). Never swap sizes on count alone — 5 × 12 mm (565.49 mm²) is not a substitute for 5 × 16 mm (1,005.3 mm²). If the new bars need longer laps or development, check the lap calculator and the development length calculator.

How do I work out steel area per metre from a spacing?

Divide the area of one bar by the spacing in metres: Ast per metre = (π/4 × d²) ÷ spacing (m). So 12 mm bars at 200 mm give 113.10 ÷ 0.2 = 565.49 mm²/m, and 16 mm bars at 150 mm give 201.06 ÷ 0.15 = 1,340.4 mm²/m. The per-metre table below covers 8–16 mm at 150–300 mm. To go the other way — spacing from a required area per metre — use spacing = one-bar area × 1000 ÷ required Ast per metre.

What if my section has two different bar sizes?

This calculator takes one bar size per run, so run it once for each size and add the two Ast figures. A beam with 2 × 20 mm top bars and 3 × 16 mm bottom bars is 628.32 + 603.19 = 1,231.5 mm². The stirrup steel is separate — it is not part of Ast — so do not add it here; use the stirrup calculator for ties.

What is the minimum steel area?

It depends on the member, and the percentages divide by different areas — see the minimum-steel section below for the full table. In short: beams need at least 0.85/fy of b × d (192.67 mm² for a 230 × 409 mm section in Fe415); columns need 0.8–6% of gross area; walls need 0.12% vertical and 0.20% horizontal of gross area per metre; slabs need 0.12% of gross area for HYSD bars (120 mm²/m on a 100 mm slab). Passing the minimum is not a design — it only catches gross errors.

Does this calculator give the weight of the steel?

No — it gives area only, not weight, length, cost or tonnage. To turn an area into an order, take the bar count and length to the weight calculator (4 × 12 mm bars weigh 4 × 0.887 = 3.55 kg per metre of member length), then to the cost calculator and the tonnage calculator. For a full count-and-length take-off from a slab or grid, use the quantity calculator.
