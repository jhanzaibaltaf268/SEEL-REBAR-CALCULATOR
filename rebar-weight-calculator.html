How to use: Rebar Weight Calculator

  1. Pick the bar size — metric (mm) or US (# size) — from the dropdown.
  2. For a known batch, leave the mode on Standard, enter the length of one bar in metres and the number of pieces.
  3. Read the total in kg, lb, metric tons and US short tons, plus the weight of one piece.
  4. For a known weight, switch the mode to Reverse, enter the total weight and your stock bar length to get total length and bars needed.
  5. Add waste, laps and hooks before ordering — the calculator weighs straight bars only.

Formula

Weight per metre (kg) = d² ÷ 162.28, where d is the bar diameter in mm. Total = weight/m × length × quantity.

The formula, explained

A bar is a long cylinder, so its weight per metre is its cross-sectional area times steel density. Area = π/4 × d² (d in mm), density = 7850 kg/m³, and converting mm² to m² divides by 1,000,000:

Weight per metre (kg/m) = π/4 × d² × 7850 ÷ 1,000,000 = d² ÷ 162.28; Total = weight/m × length × quantity

The constant falls out of the arithmetic: π/4 × 7850 ÷ 1,000,000 = 0.0061654, and 1 ÷ 0.0061654 = 162.2 — rounded to 162 for site arithmetic, kept as 162.28 by this calculator. That is the entire difference between the tables you will find online: d²/162 gives 0.889 kg/m for 12 mm, d²/162.2 gives 0.888, d²/162.28 gives 0.887. Two display notes. First, the bar dropdown reads "0.888|12", but the panel computes with the option value 0.887 kg/m, so every weight below uses 0.887 — the raw field string would give 1,065.6 kg on the defaults, not the panel's 1,064.4 kg. Second, totals at or above 1000 show one decimal with separators, so the defaults total reads "1,064.4 kg", not 1064.40 kg, while tonnages show two decimals (1.06 metric ton, 1.17 US ton). Quote the panel forms and your reader's check will match. The imperial column converts with lb/ft = kg/m × 0.671969, which round-trips to the ASTM lb/ft figures for US bars.

Worked example 1: defaults (100 × 12 mm × 12 m)

Open the calculator as it loads: Standard mode, 12 mm bars, length 12 m, quantity 100 pieces.

Unit weight = 12² ÷ 162.28 = 0.887 kg/m (0.596 lb/ft). Weight per piece = 0.887 × 12 = 10.64 kg (23.47 lb).

Total = 10.64 × 100 = 1,064.4 kg (2,346.6 lb), 1.06 metric tons, 1.17 US short tons. The panel shows all six rows: total 1,064.4 kg, imperial 2,346.6 lb, per piece 10.64 kg (23.47 lb), unit 0.887 kg/m (0.596 lb/ft), metric tonnage 1.06, US short tons 1.17, with the note "0.887 kg/m (0.596 lb/ft) × 12m × 100 pcs = 1,064.4 kg (2,346.6 lb)". Two things to notice. The metric and US tonnages differ by 10% for the same steel (1000 kg vs 907.18 kg per ton) — never compare a price per ton without asking which ton. And the per-piece figure is the one to memorise: one 12 m 12 mm bar is 10.64 kg on this panel, not the 10.67 kg the d²/162 tables quote.

Worked example 2: short bars, small order

Set Standard mode, 16 mm bars, length 6 m, quantity 50 pieces.

Unit weight = 16² ÷ 162.28 = 1.578 kg/m (1.06 lb/ft). Weight per piece = 1.578 × 6 = 9.47 kg (20.87 lb).

Total = 9.47 × 50 = 473.40 kg (1,043.7 lb), 0.473 metric tons, 0.52 US short tons. Same method, second run: 10 mm bars, 6 m, 200 pieces → per piece 3.70 kg (8.15 lb), total 739.20 kg (1,629.7 lb), 0.739 t, 0.81 US ton. Short bars change nothing in the method — length is length — but they change the order: 6 m bars fit smaller trucks and tighter sites, and the extra laps they need on long runs are not in either total (see the order section).

Worked example 3: reverse mode (2000 kg)

Switch the mode to Reverse, leave the bar at 12 mm, enter total weight 2000 kg and stock length 12 m.

Total length = 2000 ÷ 0.887 = 2,254.8 m (7,397.6 ft). Standard bars needed = ceil(2,254.8 ÷ 12) = 188 bars; full bars = 187, plus a 10.79 m offcut.

2000 kg of 12 mm bar is 2,254.8 m, or 188 stock bars — and the panel says so in both forms. The panel shows 2,254.8 m, 7,397.6 ft, 188 bars (12 m stock), 187 bars + 10.79 m, unit 0.887 kg/m (0.596 lb/ft), entered 2000 kg (4,409.2 lb) and 2.00 metric tons. Now the ceil nuance, which no competitor states: ordering 188 full bars weighs 188 × 10.64 = 2,001.07 kg, slightly MORE than the 2000 kg entered, because the offcut must still be bought as a whole bar. Order only the 187 full bars and you are 10.79 m short. Two more reverse runs: 5000 kg of 16 mm at 12 m stock → 3,168.6 m (10,395.6 ft), 265 bars, 264 + 0.57 m (ordered weight 5,018.04 kg); 1000 kg of 10 mm at 6 m stock → 1,623.4 m (5,326.0 ft), 271 bars, 270 + 3.38 m.

From neat total to order

The panel total is neat straight-bar steel. An order needs three additions, all worked here from the panel totals (exact totals throughout, not rounded display values).

Waste. Defaults neat 1,064.4 kg → 1,117.62 kg at +5%, 1,170.84 kg at +10%. Published takeoff guides use 10% for laps and waste combined; one bar-schedule method uses 3–5% for cutting alone. Pick the allowance that matches how well your cut lengths fit the stock bars — a schedule of 3 m cuts from 12 m bars wastes almost nothing, while a schedule of 5 m cuts leaves 2 m offcuts everywhere.

Cost. At an example €1.20/kg (prices are examples — enter your local rate on the cost calculator): defaults €1,277.28 neat, €1,341.14 at +5%, €1,405.01 at +10%. Convert the final figure on the tonnage calculator if your supplier prices per ton.

Trucks. A standard flatbed payload is about 20,000 kg (20 metric tons) — derived arithmetic, not a panel output, so treat it as a planning figure: the defaults order is one truck, a 25 mm bundle order of 4,621.2 kg is one truck, and anything over 20 tonnes needs a second. But length binds before weight does: 12 m bars need a 40 ft (12.2 m) trailer or longer, while 6 m bars fit shorter urban trucks — one published logistics analysis notes that specifying 6 m bars with site laps can cut delivery cost even though the laps add 5–10% tonnage.

Cross-check against a competitor's worked example: one multi-row tool works 40 × 12 mm × 6 m + 5% as 224 kg on d²/162. On this panel the same order is 212.88 kg neat and 223.52 kg at +5% — 0.48 kg lower, purely the constant. If your estimate and your supplier's differ by that kind of margin, the constant is the first place to look, not the arithmetic.

Checking a delivery

Theoretical weight is a budget baseline, not a delivery guarantee. Check steel the way site engineers do, with the panel's reverse mode:

Count first. A delivery note claiming 3000 kg of 12 mm 12 m bars should contain 282 bars (reverse mode: 3,382.2 m, 281 full + 10.19 m). If you count 265, either the bars are lighter than nominal or the weight on the bill is wrong — either way, stop and check before signing.

Weigh a sample. Pick 5 bars at random and weigh them together on a platform scale. Expected: 5 × 10.644 = 53.22 kg. If the scale shows 52.4 kg, the bars are 1.5% light — inside tolerance, but worth recording with the date, bundle tag and supplier.

Know the tolerance before you complain. Published manufacturing tolerances (firsthand tables): ASTM A615 ±6% on #3–#5 and ±4% on #6–#18; EN 10080/ISO 6935 ±6% up to 8 mm and ±4.5% above; AS/NZS 4671 ±4% on the batch mean. A 1.5% shortfall is not a claim; a 7% shortfall on 12 mm bar is. And settle one thing on the purchase order itself: whether invoicing is on theoretical weight or actual (weighed) weight — on a 50 tonne order, tolerance alone can move the delivered weight by tonnes, and the two bases will never agree to the kilogram.

What this calculator does not do

This is a bar-weight tool, not a schedule or ordering tool. It does not add laps, hooks, bends, cranks or development — see the lap calculator, the hook calculator and the development length calculator. It does not add waste or cost — the order section shows the arithmetic; price the final figure on the cost calculator and convert it on the tonnage calculator. It does not take off a whole member or building — use the member tools (slab, footing, mat, column, beam, wall, pool, driveway) or the quantity calculator for area-density estimates and the concrete estimator for kg/m³-ratio estimates. It does not run multi-size schedules in one pass (one size per run — run each size separately and add, as the area page does for mixed cages), count bars from a spacing layout (see the spacing calculator), weigh stirrups (see the stirrup calculator), chairs (see the chair calculator) or tie wire (see the tie wire calculator), or model stainless/alloy densities, mesh, or cut-length optimisation against stock bars.

Common mistakes

Mixing the constants. Using a d²/162 table against a d²/162.28 calculator (or the reverse) shifts every total by ~0.2%: 0.48 kg on the 224 kg worked example above, about 2.1 kg per tonne on 12 mm. Pick one constant and use it everywhere in a schedule.

Confusing metric tons and US short tons. The defaults total is 1.06 metric tons but 1.17 US short tons. A price per short ton looks ~10% cheaper than the same price per metric ton — and a supplier quoting tons without saying which one is quoting two different quantities.

Quoting bars per tonne without a length. One tonne of 12 mm is 94 twelve-metre bars but 92 forty-foot bars. A bars-per-tonne figure without a bar length is incomplete, and using the wrong one mis-orders by 2 bars per tonne before waste.

Ordering the full bars from reverse mode. Reverse mode's "full bars" for 1000 kg of 12 mm is 93 bars — but the tonne also contains an 11.4 m offcut, so the order is 94 bars. Ordering 93 leaves you 11.4 m short; the ceil exists for exactly this reason.

Weighing by count alone, or by member length. Counting bars without weighing a sample misses light bars entirely, and weighing member lengths instead of cut lengths misses hooks, bends, anchorage and laps — one published lap analysis puts laps alone at 150–250 kg on a 12-column house. Work from cut lengths, then count, then weigh.

Rebar Weight Calculator — FAQ

What is the weight of a 12mm rebar?

12² ÷ 162.28 = 0.887 kg per metre on this calculator, so one 12 m bar weighs 10.64 kg and 100 of them weigh 1,064.4 kg. You will also see 0.888 kg/m quoted widely — that comes from the d²/162.2 shortcut (144 ÷ 162.2 = 0.888), and 0.889 kg/m from d²/162. The difference is about 0.2%: on a tonne order of 12 mm it is roughly 2 kg. Always check which constant a table uses before comparing totals.

How do you calculate the weight of steel rebar?

Square the diameter in mm, divide by 162.28 to get kg/m, then multiply by length and quantity. For 100 bars of 12 mm at 12 m: 0.887 × 12 × 100 = 1,064.4 kg. In pounds, multiply kg by 2.20462 (1,064.4 kg = 2,346.6 lb); in metric tons divide by 1000 (1.06 t). The formula section below derives the 162.28 constant from steel density.

What is the difference between d²/162 and d²/162.28?

162 is the rounded constant; 162.28 is the precise one. Steel at 7850 kg/m³ gives weight per metre = π/4 × d² × 7850 ÷ 1,000,000 = 0.0061654 × d², and 1 ÷ 0.0061654 = 162.2 — rounded to 162 for site arithmetic, kept as 162.28 by this calculator. On 12 mm bar that is 0.889 vs 0.887 kg/m, 10.67 vs 10.64 kg per 12 m bar, and about 2.1 kg on a tonne order. Neither is wrong; they are different roundings, and mixing them is how two estimates for the same steel disagree.

How many bars are in a tonne?

It depends on the bar size AND the bar length — a figure without a length is meaningless. For 12 m stock bars, one tonne needs: 8 mm 212 bars (211 full + 6.07 m offcut), 10 mm 136 (135 + 3.38 m), 12 mm 94 (93 + 11.4 m), 16 mm 53 (52 + 9.71 m), 20 mm 34 (33 + 9.68 m), 25 mm 22 (21 + 7.67 m), 32 mm 14 (13 + 2.48 m). For 40 ft (12.192 m) bars the counts are lower — 12 mm is 92, not 94 — because each bar is longer. The per-tonne table below gives the full reverse-mode figures.

What does a bundle of rebar weigh?

Bundle sizes vary by supplier, so weigh by count, not by the word bundle: 100 bars of 12 m weigh 266.40 kg (6 mm), 472.80 kg (8 mm), 739.20 kg (10 mm), 1,064.4 kg (12 mm), 1,893.6 kg (16 mm), 2,958 kg (20 mm), 4,621.2 kg (25 mm) and 7,572 kg (32 mm). One published supplier chart packs 10 × 8 mm, 7 × 10 mm, 5 × 12 mm, 3 × 16 mm, 2 × 20 mm and 1 × 25/32 mm per bundle — those piece counts are that supplier's, not a standard. Always confirm pieces per bundle on your delivery note.

How do I work backwards from a weight to bars?

Switch the calculator to Reverse mode, enter the weight and your stock length. For 2000 kg of 12 mm bar at 12 m stock it gives 2,254.8 m of bar (7,397.6 ft), 188 bars needed — 187 full bars plus a 10.79 m offcut. Note the ceil: ordering 188 full bars weighs 2,001.07 kg, slightly MORE than the 2000 kg you entered, because the offcut still has to be bought as a whole bar. If you order only the 187 full bars you will be 10.79 m short.

Does the bar grade change the weight?

No. Fe415, Fe500, Fe550 and Grade 60 differ in strength, not density, so the weight per metre is the same for a given diameter — and the same holds for TMT bars, which are heat-treated but still 7850 kg/m³ steel. Stainless reinforcement is the exception (about 7900–8000 kg/m³, roughly 1% heavier), and it is not what this calculator models. Grade affects laps, development and design — see the lap calculator and the development length calculator — not weight.

Why does delivered steel weigh differently from the table?

Because the table is theoretical (nominal diameter, exact density) and real bars vary within manufacturing tolerance: ASTM A615 allows ±6% on #3–#5 and ±4% on #6–#18, EN 10080/ISO 6935 allows ±6% up to 8 mm and ±4.5% above, and AS/NZS 4671 allows ±4% on the batch mean. On a 50 tonne order that compounds to tonnes, not kilograms. Check deliveries the way site engineers do: count the bars against the reverse-mode figure (3000 kg of 12 mm 12 m bars should be 282 bars — 281 full + 10.19 m), then weigh a sample (5 × 12 m 12 mm bars should be 53.22 kg). If the gap exceeds the applicable tolerance, raise it with the supplier — and check whether your order prices theoretical or actual (weighed) weight.

What is the difference between a metric ton and a US short ton?

A metric ton (tonne) is 1000 kg; a US short ton is 2000 lb = 907.18 kg — about 10% smaller. On the calculator defaults (100 × 12 mm × 12 m) that is 1.06 metric tons but 1.17 US short tons, for the same steel. Suppliers quoting tons without saying which one are quoting two different quantities: a price per short ton looks about 10% cheaper than the same price per metric ton. Convert any quote on the tonnage calculator before comparing.

Does the calculator include waste, laps, hooks or cost?

No — it weighs straight bars only. Add 5–10% for cutting and laps (published takeoff guides use 10% for laps and waste combined; one bar-schedule method uses 3–5% for cutting alone): the defaults total goes from 1,064.4 kg neat to 1,117.62 kg at +5% and 1,170.84 kg at +10%, which costs €1,277.28 / €1,341.14 / €1,405.01 at an example €1.20/kg. Work laps out on the lap calculator, hooks on the hook calculator, price the result on the cost calculator, and build a full takeoff on the quantity calculator.
