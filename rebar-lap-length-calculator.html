How to use: Rebar Lap / Splice Length Calculator

  1. Pick the bar size.
  2. Select the steel grade (Fe250–Fe550) and concrete grade (M20–M40).
  3. Choose tension or compression lap.
  4. Read the lap length in mm, as a multiple of diameter, plus the development length and the code minimum — the note under the result shows which value governed.
  5. Apply the staggering and placement rules below before detailing — the calculator gives the length, not the location.

Formula

Tension lap = max(Ld, 30d); compression lap = max(Ld, 24d), where Ld is the development length.

The formula, explained

Every lap length starts from the development length Ld — the embedment a bar needs to develop its full design stress in concrete (IS 456:2000 Cl 26.2.1):

Ld = (d × 0.87 × fy) ÷ (4 × τbd)

  • d is the bar diameter in mm.
  • 0.87 × fy is the design stress in the steel. The 0.87 is 1 ÷ 1.15, the partial safety factor for steel — using fy itself overstates Ld by 15%.
  • τbd is the design bond stress. The table below gives plain-bar values; deformed bars (the usual site bar) raise them by 60%, and compression raises them a further 25% — which is why a compression Ld is 0.8 × the tension Ld.

Then the lap rule (IS 456 Cl 26.2.5.1) takes the greater of Ld and a code floor:

  • Tension lap = max(Ld, 30d) — flexural tension, beams and slabs.
  • Compression lap = max(Ld, 24d) — columns and other compression members.

The panel note shows which value governed, e.g. "lap = max(Ld 583, 30d 360) mm" for the defaults — Ld rounded to a whole number in the note, exact values in the result tiles.

Worked example 1: 12 mm, Fe500, M25, tension (calculator defaults)

Open the calculator and it already shows this case: 12 mm bar, Fe500 steel, M25 concrete, tension lap.

  • τbd = 1.40 × 1.6 = 2.24 N/mm² (deformed bars).
  • Ld = (12 × 0.87 × 500) ÷ (4 × 2.24) = 5220 ÷ 8.96 = 582.59 mm.
  • Code minimum = 30 × 12 = 360.00 mm.
  • Lap = max(582.59, 360.00) = 582.59 mm = 48.55 × d.

Try it yourself: the panel opens on 582.59 mm, 48.55 × d, Ld 582.59 mm, minimum 360.00 mm.

Worked example 2: the same bars in compression

Switch the lap type to Compression and keep 12 mm, Fe500, M25:

  • τbd = 2.24 × 1.25 = 2.80 N/mm² (compression bond stress).
  • Ld = 5220 ÷ (4 × 2.80) = 5220 ÷ 11.20 = 466.07 mm.
  • Code minimum = 24 × 12 = 288.00 mm.
  • Lap = max(466.07, 288.00) = 466.07 mm = 38.84 × d.

That is exactly 0.8 × the tension lap (0.8 × 582.59 = 466.07) — the compression saving comes from the higher bond stress, not from a shorter rule. Column bars that also carry bending can go into tension under lateral loads, so many designers detail column laps as tension laps to be safe; always follow the structural drawings.

Worked example 3: when the code minimum governs

Take a mild-steel bar in strong concrete: 12 mm, Fe250, M40, tension.

  • τbd = 3.04 N/mm²; Ld = (12 × 0.87 × 250) ÷ (4 × 3.04) = 2610 ÷ 12.16 = 214.64 mm.
  • Code minimum = 30 × 12 = 360.00 mm.
  • Lap = max(214.64, 360.00) = 360.00 mm = 30.00 × d.

The floor exists so that even a short Ld still gets a workable overlap. The same thing happens at Fe415/M40: tension Ld is 356.30 mm but the lap is 360.00 mm, and compression Ld is 285.04 mm but the lap is 288.00 mm. If you ever see a lap quoted below 30d in tension or 24d in compression, it is wrong unless a specific code clause says otherwise.

Staggering and placement rules

The calculator gives the length. These rules govern where laps go (IS 456 Cl 26.2.5, IS 13920 for seismic detailing):

  • Stagger: splices count as staggered only when successive splices are at least 1.3 × the lap length apart, centre to centre — 757.37 mm for example 1, 605.89 mm for example 2. Never lap all bars at one section; not more than half the bars should be spliced at any one section.
  • Straight portion: the straight length of a lap must be at least max(15d, 200 mm) — 200 mm for a 12 mm bar, 240 mm for a 16 mm bar.
  • Columns: laps in the middle third of the column height, away from beam–column joints and the foundation interface.
  • Beams: bottom bars lap near the supports, top bars near mid-span — never in the zone of maximum moment.
  • Slabs and footings: keep laps away from the highest-moment regions (mid-span for bottom steel, column face for footing mats) and stagger them across the mat.
  • Different diameters: calculate on the smaller diameter.
  • Large bars: no lap splices above 32 mm — use welding or mechanical couplers (older references say 36 mm; that was the pre-2007 limit).
  • Top bars and corners: a tension lap at the top of a section with cover below 2d, or at a corner with tight spacing, needs a 1.4 factor — about 816 mm for example 1. If both conditions apply, the factor is 2.0. The calculator does not apply these factors; multiply its result yourself.
  • Binding: wire every lap at both ends so the bars stay in contact during concreting — a loose lap is a failed lap.

Laps in the bar schedule: count, weight and cost

Laps are not free steel — they must be in the cutting lengths, or the site runs short.

  • Count the laps: pieces = ceil(run ÷ 12 m stock), laps per line = pieces − 1. A 25 m run needs 3 pieces, so 2 laps per line.
  • Extra length: laps per line × lap length. At the calculator defaults, 2 × 582.59 = 1165.18 mm per line; over 10 lines that is 11.65 m of extra bar.
  • Extra weight: 12 mm bar is 0.887 kg/m, so one default splice weighs 0.52 kg and 100 splices weigh 51.68 kg.
  • Extra cost: at €1.20/kg (example price — get a current quote), 100 default splices cost €62.01 in steel alone, before cutting waste. Add a 2–5% waste allowance on top of the lapped tonnage, not instead of it.

For a full count-and-length take-off, use the quantity & takeoff calculator; for the cost breakdown, the rebar cost calculator.

What the calculator does not include

  • Direct tension laps. Ties and hangers need max(2Ld, 30d) — 1165.18 mm for the defaults. Double the Ld yourself.
  • Top-bar and corner factors. ×1.4 for cover below 2d or tight corners, ×2.0 if both apply.
  • Staggering and placement. The 1.3 × lap stagger rule and the member placement rules above are detailing decisions, not outputs.
  • Plain bars. The calculator assumes deformed bars. For plain bars, divide τbd by 1.6 and recompute Ld by hand.
  • Hooks, welds and couplers. Hooks anchor bar ends (see the hook & bend calculator); welded and mechanical splices follow their own rules and are the only option above 32 mm.
  • ACI 318 splices. Class A (1.0 × Ld) vs Class B (1.3 × Ld) tension splices, and the ACI compression splice formulas, are a different code system — this calculator is IS 456.
  • Seismic detailing. IS 13920 adds confinement and location rules for seismic zones; follow the project drawings.
  • Lap counts and weights. The calculator gives one splice length, not the total laps, weight or cost for a member — see the bar-schedule section above.

Common mistakes

  1. Using a fixed 50d for everything. 50d approximates Fe415/M20 tension (47.01d) and nothing else. On M40 it wastes steel; on weak concrete with Fe500 it under-laps by up to 12% (56.64d needed at M20). Calculate per grade.
  2. Using the tension lap in compression, or vice versa. Compression laps are 0.8 × tension laps at the same grades. Using the compression value in a tension zone under-laps the splice by 20%.
  3. Using plain-bar bond stress for deformed bars. That understates τbd by 60% and overstates the lap by 60% — or, copied the other way, understates the real requirement. This calculator already applies the 60% increase.
  4. Forgetting the 30d/24d floor. Lap is max(Ld, floor), not Ld alone. At Fe250/M40 the Ld is only 214.64 mm but the lap is still 360.00 mm.
  5. Lapping every bar at the same section, or at peak moment. Bunched laps create a weak plane and congested steel that concrete cannot flow around. Stagger at least 1.3 × the lap apart and keep laps out of maximum-moment zones.

Rebar Lap / Splice Length Calculator — FAQ

What is lap length of rebar?

The overlap between two spliced bars so force transfers from one bar to the other through the concrete bond. Bars come in standard lengths (usually 12 m), so any longer run must be continued by lapping the next bar over the lap length.

Is lap length 40d or 50d?

It depends on grade and concrete — commonly 40–50d, but that is a rule of thumb, not a code value. This tool computes it from Ld: for 12 mm Fe500 bars in M25 concrete the tension lap is 48.55 × d (582.59 mm), while for Fe415 in M20 it is 47.01 × d. Always calculate for your actual grades — see the tables below.

Why is the compression lap shorter than the tension lap?

Two reasons. First, the design bond stress in compression is 25% higher than in tension (IS 456 Cl 26.2.1.1), so the compression development length is 0.8 × the tension value — 466.07 mm vs 582.59 mm for 12 mm Fe500/M25. Second, the code floor is lower: 24d in compression vs 30d in tension.

What is the difference between development length and lap length?

Development length (Ld) anchors one bar into concrete so it develops its full strength; lap length overlaps two bars so force transfers from one to the other. A lap is built on Ld — tension lap = max(Ld, 30d), compression lap = max(Ld, 24d) — but lap rules add their own minimums and staggering requirements. For Ld alone, use the development length calculator.

Where should laps be placed?

Away from maximum-moment zones and staggered, never all at one section. Column laps belong in the middle third of the height, away from beam–column joints; beam bottom bars lap near the supports and top bars near mid-span; no more than half the bars should be spliced at any one section. Successive splices count as staggered only when they are at least 1.3 × the lap length apart.

What about direct tension — ties and hangers?

Direct tension is stricter: IS 456 Cl 26.2.5.1 requires max(2Ld, 30d). For the calculator defaults (12 mm, Fe500, M25) that is 1165.18 mm — double the 582.59 mm flexural-tension lap. This calculator covers flexural tension and compression only, so double the Ld yourself for direct-tension members.

Can bars larger than 32 mm be lap spliced?

No. IS 456:2000 as amended in 2007 does not permit lap splices for bars larger than 32 mm — use welding or mechanical couplers instead. Older references cite 36 mm; that was the pre-amendment limit. A 32 mm lap would already be over a metre of doubled steel, which is why the code draws the line there.

How many laps do I need, and how much extra steel?

Divide the run by the 12 m stock length and round up: pieces = ceil(run ÷ 12), laps per line = pieces − 1. A 25 m run needs 3 pieces, so 2 laps per line — at the calculator defaults that adds 2 × 582.59 = 1165.18 mm per line, or 11.65 m over 10 lines. Put laps in the cutting lengths, or the site runs short.

What if the two spliced bars have different diameters?

Calculate on the smaller diameter. The bond transfer is limited by the smaller bar, so using the larger diameter would overstate the lap. The same applies when a bar laps onto a starter dowel of a different size.

Does the calculator include hooks, welds or couplers?

No — it gives straight lap lengths only. A hook anchors a bar end; it does not replace a lap, and a lapped bar still needs its lap even if it is hooked. For hook and bend allowances use the hook & bend calculator; for bars over 32 mm, or where congestion rules laps out, price mechanical couplers against the lapped steel they replace.
