How to use: Swimming Pool Rebar Calculator

  1. Enter one wall panel's length and height in metres, and the edge cover in mm.
  2. Set the vertical spacing and bar size, and the horizontal spacing and bar size.
  3. Read the bar counts and weights for that one panel — vertical steel, horizontal steel and the total.
  4. Repeat for each wall (long and short sides usually differ), then add the floor using the slab tool.
  5. Add laps, bond beam, steps and waste before ordering — the calculator counts straight, cover-reduced bars for one panel only.

Formula

Same grid method as a wall: bars each way from spacing, summed × kg/m. Repeat per wall and add the floor.

The formula, explained

A pool wall panel is a wall grid: verticals counted along the length, horizontals counted up the height, and the calculator keeps them separate so you can see which direction dominates:

Vertical bars = floor((L − 2cover) ÷ spV) + 1, each (H − 2cover) long; Horizontal bars = floor((H − 2cover) ÷ spH) + 1, each (L − 2cover) long; Total = vertical steel + horizontal steel

Both counts use the same fence-post rule as every grid on this site: the cover-reduced run divided by the spacing gives the number of intervals, and the bars number one more than the intervals, because both end bars count. And both bar lengths are cover-reduced — the verticals stop 50 mm short of the top and bottom on the defaults, the horizontals 50 mm short of each end. One display note, carried over from the wall page: both bar fields read "0.617|10", but the panel computes with the option value 0.616 kg/m, so every weight below uses 0.616. And one counting note that is new on this page: when the cover-reduced run divides EXACTLY by the spacing, the panel counts one bar fewer than a hand calculation gives (floating-point arithmetic lands just below the integer). The horizontal-spacing table below shows both instances — trust the panel count, not the hand count.

Worked example 1: defaults (8 × 1.5 m panel)

Open the calculator and leave the defaults: length 8 m, height 1.5 m, vertical spacing 250 mm, horizontal spacing 250 mm, edge cover 50 mm, vertical bars 10 mm, horizontal bars 10 mm.

Verticals = floor((8 − 0.10) ÷ 0.25) + 1 = floor(31.6) + 1 = 32 bars, each 1.5 − 0.10 = 1.40 m long → 32 × 1.40 × 0.616 = 27.60 kg. Horizontals = floor((1.5 − 0.10) ÷ 0.25) + 1 = floor(5.6) + 1 = 6 bars, each 8 − 0.10 = 7.90 m long → 6 × 7.90 × 0.616 = 29.20 kg.

Total = 27.60 + 29.20 = 56.80 kg (0.057 t). The panel shows 56.80 kg, 32.00 vertical bars, 6.00 horizontal bars, 27.60 kg vertical steel, 29.20 kg horizontal steel and 0.057 t. Note the split: on this long, shallow panel the horizontals (51.4%) outweigh the verticals — length dominates when height is small, the opposite of a tall wall. Total bar length is 32 × 1.40 + 6 × 7.90 = 92.20 m, and at 12 m² of panel face that is 4.73 kg/m² — a useful density to compare against the next examples. And remember: this is ONE panel. A pool has four of them plus a floor.

Worked example 2: 10 × 2 m panel

Set length 10 m, height 2 m, vertical spacing 200 mm, horizontal spacing 200 mm, cover 50 mm, vertical bars 12 mm, horizontal bars 12 mm.

Verticals = floor((10 − 0.10) ÷ 0.20) + 1 = 50 bars, each 2 − 0.10 = 1.90 m → 50 × 1.90 × 0.887 = 84.27 kg. Horizontals = floor((2 − 0.10) ÷ 0.20) + 1 = 10 bars, each 10 − 0.10 = 9.90 m → 10 × 9.90 × 0.887 = 87.81 kg.

Total = 84.27 + 87.81 = 172.08 kg (0.172 t). At 20 m² of panel face that is 8.60 kg/m² — nearly double the defaults density, because both spacings tightened and both bar sizes stepped up at once. Note the horizontal bars here run 9.90 m, still inside a 12 m stock length — no splice needed. Stretch this panel to 15 m and that stops being true (see the laps section).

Worked example 3: small plunge pool panel

Set length 6 m, height 1.2 m, vertical spacing 300 mm, horizontal spacing 300 mm, cover 40 mm, vertical bars 10 mm, horizontal bars 10 mm.

Verticals = floor((6 − 0.08) ÷ 0.30) + 1 = 20 bars, each 1.2 − 0.08 = 1.12 m → 20 × 1.12 × 0.616 = 13.80 kg. Horizontals = floor((1.2 − 0.08) ÷ 0.30) + 1 = 4 bars, each 6 − 0.08 = 5.92 m → 4 × 5.92 × 0.616 = 14.59 kg.

Total = 13.80 + 14.59 = 28.39 kg (0.028 t). At 7.2 m² that is only 3.94 kg/m² — a lightly reinforced shallow panel, and the 300 mm spacings are fine only if the drawing says so: published pool practice runs 12–16 in (about 300–400 mm) for many residential shells, but deeper water, heavier soil and structural walls all tighten the grid. A small pool is not automatically a lightly reinforced pool — depth and soil decide, not size.

Vertical spacing

Same defaults panel (8 × 1.5 m, 10 mm horizontals @ 250 mm giving a constant 29.20 kg), five vertical spacings. The horizontal steel never moves, so the whole table is the vertical half doing its thing.

Two readings. First, spacing is a strong lever: tightening from 250 mm to 150 mm adds 21 verticals and 18.11 kg (+32%) to one panel. Second, there are dead bands: any vertical spacing from 247 mm to 254 mm gives the same 32 bars on this panel — 254 mm returns exactly the defaults total of 56.80 kg — while 255 mm drops to 31 bars and 55.93 kg. So a spacing "improvement" of a few millimetres often changes nothing. Always read the count, not just the spacing. And one pool-specific limit: for gunite/shotcrete shells, published pool-engineering practice caps shell bars at #5 and spacings at no tighter than 2-1/2 in, so pneumatically applied concrete can fill behind the steel — a 150 mm spacing is fine, but do not chase tighter grids without checking the shell can actually be shot.

The 200 mm row needs a footnote, and it is the most useful row in the table. By hand, floor(1.40 ÷ 0.20) + 1 = 8 horizontals — but the panel shows 7, with 34.06 kg and a 61.66 kg total. The cover-reduced run (1.40 m) divides exactly by 200 mm, and the panel's arithmetic lands just below the integer, so it counts one bar fewer. The same thing happens on a 12 × 2.5 m panel at 200 mm horizontal spacing: by hand 13 horizontals, the panel counts 12 (87.96 kg of horizontal steel, 215.69 kg total). Neither row is a typo — both reproduce from the panel exactly as shown. The rule is simple: after changing a spacing, read the panel's bar count; do not trust a hand count when the run divides exactly by the spacing.

The minimum-steel check

The calculator weighs whatever grid you enter; it never asks whether the grid is enough. The check needs the wall thickness, which the calculator does not ask for — so here it is, worked by hand, using the IS 456 wall minima as the yardstick (pool design itself is engineer-governed; see the design FAQ). Steel per metre of panel is bar area ÷ spacing: a 10 mm bar is 78.54 mm², a 12 mm bar 113.10 mm².

On a 200 mm thick wall the defaults grid gives 78.54 ÷ 0.25 = 314.16 mm²/m each way, which is 314.16 ÷ 200000 = 0.157% — above the 0.12% vertical minimum but BELOW the 0.20% horizontal minimum. The defaults grid, as it stands, fails the horizontal check on a 200 mm wall. The fix is small: tighten the horizontals to 190 mm (78.54 ÷ 0.19 = 413.37 mm²/m = 0.207%, panel total 66.53 kg) or step the horizontal bar up to 12 mm at 250 mm (113.10 ÷ 0.25 = 452.39 mm²/m = 0.226%, panel total 69.64 kg). Either fix costs a few kilograms; the uncorrected grid costs a failed check. On a 150 mm wall the same grid passes both: 314.16 ÷ 150000 = 0.209%.

Two wider cautions. First, published pool-wall research caps crack width at 0.25 mm for clean-water pools — tighter than many structural checks — because any crack in a pool can leak; minimum steel is a floor, not a guarantee against cracking. Second, pool codes are deliberately conservative for exactly that reason: the water load never goes away, so published pool engineering puts properly designed shells at roughly twice the actual water-plus-soil load. Minimum percentages catch gross errors; they do not design the shell.

From one panel to the whole pool

The calculator gives one panel. The pool is four panels plus a floor. Worked example: an 8 × 4 m rectangular pool, 1.5 m deep, at the defaults grid (10 mm @ 250 mm each way, 50 mm cover).

Long walls: two panels of 8 × 1.5 m at 56.80 kg each = 113.59 kg. Short walls: two panels of 4 × 1.5 m at the defaults grid — 16 verticals, 6 horizontals, 13.80 + 14.41 = 28.21 kg each — = 56.43 kg. Walls total 170.02 kg. Floor: run the slab calculator for an 8 × 4 m floor at 150 mm spacing, 12 mm bars, 40 mm cover → 373.96 kg (421.60 m of bar). Shell neat total = 170.02 + 373.96 = 543.98 kg. With a 5% waste allowance that is 571.17 kg, which costs €685.41 at an example €1.20/kg (prices are examples — enter your local rate on the cost calculator).

Three things that total still omits. The bond beam: as a scale illustration only, one continuous #4 bar around the 24 m perimeter weighs 23.86 kg (two bars 47.71 kg) — take the actual bond-beam steel from the drawing. Steps, benches and any spa: published pool practice calls these structurally weaker areas needing extra steel, not less. And laps on long walls, covered next. Convert any schedule on the tonnage calculator, and build a full count-and-length takeoff on the quantity calculator.

Long walls and laps

Two additions turn a panel total into an order quantity, and both are missing from the calculator by design. First, splices: any bar run longer than stock length needs one. Take a 15 m wall at the defaults grid — 60 verticals @ 1.40 m = 51.74 kg, 6 horizontals @ 14.90 m = 55.07 kg, total 106.81 kg. The horizontal bars run 14.90 m, past even a 40 ft (12.19 m) stick, so each needs one splice. Pool practice overlaps bars at 48 × bar diameter (#3 → 18 in, #4 → 24 in, #5 → 30 in), so a 10 mm bar splices at 0.48 m, adding 6 × 0.48 × 0.616 = 1.77 kg (total with laps 108.59 kg). With 20 ft (6.10 m) sticks the same bar needs two splices: +3.55 kg. That is also why published pool guidance prefers 40 ft sticks — fewer overlaps, and every overlap in a shotcrete shell is a place where concrete can fail to fill behind the steel. Get the code lap from the lap length calculator where the drawing does not state it.

Second, the vertical bars are short (1.40 m on the defaults panel) and need no splice — but they do need to tie into the floor steel and the bond beam, and the hooks and dowels for those joints are not in this grid. Work them out on the hook cutting length calculator and the development length calculator, and add the weight. A shell schedule that ignores laps, hooks and the bond beam under-orders on every wall at once.

Imperial conversions

The calculator takes metres and millimetres, so convert first: 1 m = 3.28 ft, 1 kg = 2.20 lb, 1 in = 25.4 mm.

Worked conversions: the defaults total 56.80 kg = 125.21 lb (0.057 t); example 2 totals 172.08 kg = 379.37 lb (0.172 t). An 8 m wall is 26.25 ft long, a 1.5 m wall 4.92 ft high, 250 mm spacing is 9.84 in and 50 mm cover is 1.97 in. A full imperial run — a 25 × 5 ft wall (7.62 × 1.524 m), 10 in spacing both ways, 2 in cover, #4 verticals (12.7 mm, 0.994 kg/m) and #3 horizontals (9.5 mm, 0.56 kg/m) — gives 30 verticals and 6 horizontals, 42.42 + 25.26 = 67.68 kg (149.20 lb). The bar dropdowns include US sizes, so enter the metric equivalents of your dimensions and pick the matching US bar. Note the cover translation: published pool practice wants 2 in to water and 3 in to soil — the 2 in used here suits the water face; check the soil face against your drawing.

What this calculator does not cover

  • Structural design — water pressure, soil pressure, buoyancy, seismic loads, shell thickness, crack control and the required steel area; the minimum-steel check above catches gross errors, it does not prove the shell holds water.
  • The floor — run the slab calculator for it (see the whole-pool example).
  • The other three walls — run each panel separately; long and short walls differ.
  • Bond beam, steps, benches, spa, skimmer and light niches, plumbing penetrations — all from the drawing, none in this grid.
  • Laps, hooks, bends at corners, dowels into the floor — see the lap length calculator and the hook cutting length calculator.
  • Second grids / double mats — the calculator counts one grid per panel; if the design calls for two curtains, double the panel total.
  • Freeform and curved shells — break them into straight runs by hand; the result is an estimate, not a schedule.
  • Waterstop, hydrostatic relief, waterproofing and plaster — none of these are steel, and none are in this tool.
  • Chairs and spacers for the floor steel — see the chair calculator; tie wire is on the tie wire calculator.
  • Cost, tonnage and multi-panel takeoff — the whole-pool section shows the hand arithmetic; the cost calculator prices a full takeoff, the tonnage calculator converts it, and the quantity & takeoff calculator totals across members.

5 common mistakes

  1. Ordering one panel for the whole pool. The defaults panel is 56.80 kg; the full 8 × 4 m shell is 543.98 kg neat. The panel total is roughly a quarter of the shell — ordering it delivers about a tenth of the steel once the floor is counted. Always multiply out: four walls plus floor.
  2. Forgetting the +1 on both counts. Length ÷ spacing without the +1 gives 31 verticals instead of 32 and 5 horizontals instead of 6 on the defaults panel — 0.86 + 4.87 = 5.73 kg missing from every panel. Both end bars are real bars.
  3. Counting full lengths instead of cover-reduced lengths. Verticals run height minus twice the cover (1.40 m, not 1.50 m) and horizontals length minus twice the cover (7.90 m, not 8.00 m). Using full lengths overstates the defaults panel by 1.97 + 0.37 = 2.34 kg — steel that does not exist and bars that do not fit with their cover intact.
  4. Trusting a hand count when the run divides exactly. At 200 mm horizontal spacing on the defaults panel, the hand count says 8 horizontals but the panel counts 7 (34.06 kg, 61.66 kg total); on a 12 × 2.5 m panel at 200 mm it says 13 but the panel counts 12 (87.96 kg, 215.69 kg total). The panel's arithmetic lands just below the integer on exact divisions. After changing a spacing, read the panel's bar count — it is the number that ships.
  5. Forgetting laps, bond beam and steps. A 15 m wall's horizontal bars each need a splice (+1.77 kg at the 48d rule, +3.55 kg with 20 ft sticks); the bond beam around an 8 × 4 m pool is another ~24–48 kg of continuous bar; steps and benches need their own steel. None of it is in the panel total, and on a multi-wall shell the omission repeats on every wall.

Swimming Pool Rebar Calculator — FAQ

What size rebar for a pool?

For a residential gunite/shotcrete pool, #3–#4 (10–13 mm) at 250–300 mm centres each way is typical — the calculator defaults to 10 mm at 250 mm each way. Larger pools, deeper walls and structural (poured) pool walls often step up to #5 (16 mm) or tighter spacings, and one published pool-engineering rule caps shell bars at #5 with no spacing tighter than 2-1/2 in, so shotcrete can fill behind the bars. Follow your engineer: the drawing's bar schedule always wins over any typical size.

How do I calculate pool rebar?

Calculate each wall panel and the floor separately, then add them together. Run this calculator once per wall — the long walls and the short walls usually give different totals — and run the slab calculator for the floor. The worked whole-pool example below (8 × 4 m pool, 1.5 m deep) gives 170.02 kg of wall steel plus 373.96 kg of floor steel = 543.98 kg neat, before laps, bond beam and waste.

Does the calculator cover the whole pool?

No — it covers ONE rectangular wall panel. A rectangular pool has four walls plus a floor, and the panel total is roughly a quarter of the shell: the defaults panel is 56.80 kg, while the full 8 × 4 m shell in the example is 543.98 kg. Ordering the panel total for a whole pool is the single biggest under-order on this page. Freeform pools need one more step: break the shell into straight runs by hand and run each run as a panel.

What spacing should pool rebar be?

Commonly 250–300 mm each way for residential pools (the calculator defaults to 250 mm), and 12–16 in (about 300–400 mm) in much published pool practice — but two pool-specific limits apply. For gunite/shotcrete shells, keep bars no larger than #5 and spacings no tighter than 2-1/2 in, so pneumatically applied concrete can fill behind the steel; lapped-together bars are exactly where voids ("shadows") form. And the spacing on your drawing governs: deeper walls, heavier soil loads and structural walls all tighten it.

What cover does pool steel need?

Published pool practice calls for 2 in (about 50 mm) of concrete to the water side and 3 in (about 76 mm) to the soil side. The calculator's 50 mm default therefore matches the water face; the soil face typically needs more. The calculator has one edge-cover field for all four sides of the panel, so if your drawing specifies different covers per face, run the governing cover — or run each face's bars separately — and check the result against the drawing.

Do I need to add lap length to a pool wall?

Yes, whenever a bar run exceeds the stock length. The calculator counts every bar at its cover-reduced length with no splice added. Pool practice overlaps bars at 48 × bar diameter (#3 → 18 in, #4 → 24 in, #5 → 30 in). On a 15 m wall at the defaults grid, the six horizontal bars run 14.90 m — past even a 40 ft stick — so each needs one 0.48 m splice for 10 mm bars, adding 6 × 0.48 × 0.616 = 1.77 kg. Get the code lap from the lap length calculator where the drawing does not state it.

Does the calculator include the bond beam, steps or benches?

No. The bond beam — the continuous reinforced ring at the top of the wall that ties the shell together — is not in this grid, and neither are steps, benches, a spa, skimmer or light niches, or plumbing penetrations. As a scale illustration only, one continuous #4 bar around an 8 × 4 m pool perimeter (24 m) weighs 23.86 kg. Take all of these from the drawing; steps and benches in particular are called out in published pool practice as structurally weaker areas that need extra steel, not less.

Can I use this calculator for a freeform or round pool?

As an estimate, yes, with one translation: break the shell into straight runs and run each run as a panel, then add them. A curved wall's bars do not run in straight lengths, so the panel total is an approximation — the tighter the curve, the rougher it gets, and bars forced together around curvature need the substitution published in one municipal shotcrete spec (1 #4 for 2 #3 bars) to keep a minimum 2 in spacing for shotcrete fill. Round and oval pools without coves, spas or extensions are the most solid shapes structurally; 90° corners are the weakest and need extra steel and thickness.

Does the calculator design the pool shell?

No — it weighs a grid you describe; it does not check whether the grid is adequate. Water pressure, soil pressure, buoyancy (an empty pool in high groundwater can lift — that is what hydrostatic relief plugs are for), crack control (published pool-wall research caps crack width at 0.25 mm for clean-water pools), and the shell thickness all come from the structural design and the drawing. Pool codes are deliberately conservative — water load is constant and any crack can leak — so a total that looks reasonable is not proof the shell holds water.

Does the calculator include waste, tie wire or cost?

No — it gives the neat one-panel total only. Add 5–10% waste for cutting and laps (one published grid guide recommends 10% for overlaps, cutoffs and waste), estimate the fixing wire on the tie wire calculator, price the order with the cost calculator, and check the steel tonnage with the tonnage calculator. For a full takeoff across members use the quantity & takeoff calculator.
