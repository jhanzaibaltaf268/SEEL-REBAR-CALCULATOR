Rebar Cost Calculator

Turn a steel weight into a price. Enter the total weight, your price per kg and a wastage allowance, and the tool returns the estimated cost including waste and the per-ton rate.

Result

–

How to use it

  1. Get the total neat weight first — from the weight calculator, the tonnage calculator, or a takeoff on the quantity calculator.
  2. Enter the total weight in kilograms.
  3. Enter your supplier's price per kg — divide a per-tonne quote by 1000 first.
  4. Set the wastage percentage (the default is 3%; use 5–10% for bent or complex work).
  5. Read the estimated cost, the weight including wastage, the cost per ton and the tonnage — then add tax, delivery and labour separately, since the panel prices material only.

Formula

Cost = weight × (1 + wastage%) × price per kg.

Questions

How much does rebar cost?

It varies by market and size; enter your local price per kg for an accurate estimate. The panel itself quotes no market price — its default rate of 0.85 per kg is only a placeholder, and the rate table below shows what the same tonne costs at 0.50, 0.75, 0.85, 1.00, 1.20, 1.50 and 2.00 per kg. Always get a current supplier quote: one published estimating guide lists a typical input range of 0.5–3.0 per kg, which is a range, not a price. And check the quote's basis — per kg, per metric tonne, or per US short ton — before comparing (see the conversions section).

What wastage should I allow for rebar?

Typically 3–5% to cover offcuts and laps — that is the panel's default of 3% and the live guidance it has always carried. Refine it by structure, from published guidance: columns 3–5%, footings 5–7%, slabs 5–7%, walls 5–7%, beams 5–8%, stairs 8–10% (complex shapes, multiple bends). Straight bars need the least; bent bars, hooks and tight cutting plans need the most. One caution: if your takeoff already includes laps (from the lap calculator), the waste percentage should cover cutting and handling only — adding a combined 10% on top of explicit laps double-counts. The waste section below works both cases.

Does the cost include labour, delivery or tax?

No — the panel prices material only: weight × (1 + waste) × rate. Everything else sits outside it: cutting and bending charges, binding wire and accessories (chairs, spacers, cover blocks — see the chair calculator and the tie wire calculator), delivery and transport, and tax/VAT. Real rate build-ups separate these lines — one published analysis sheet, for example, builds a per-kg rate from material + labour + 15% overhead and profit — so a material-only figure must never be presented as an installed price. Add the other lines by hand after the panel.

What does "Cost per ton" mean on the panel?

It is your rate × 1000 — the price per tonne of steel you BUY, including the waste allowance, not per tonne you NEED. On the defaults (1000 kg, 0.85/kg, 3%) it reads 850.00, and indeed 875.50 ÷ 1.03 t = 850.00. But the cost per tonne of neat steel is 875.50 ÷ 1.00 = 875.50, because you pay for the 30 kg of waste too. When a supplier quotes a per-tonne rate, ask whether it is per tonne ordered or per tonne in the structure — the two differ by exactly the waste percentage.

Why does the panel show 1,341.1 when my multiplication gives 1,341.14?

Because the panel rounds totals at or above 1000 to one decimal: 1064.4 kg × 1.05 × 1.20 = 1,341.144 exactly, which displays as "1,341.1". Same money, panel rounding — and the same applies to the weight page's bridge figures, which show the exact 2-decimal products (€1,341.14 and €1,405.01) for the same orders that read 1,341.1 and 1,405 here. Below 1000 the panel shows two decimals, so the quantity page's €388.08 and €406.56 match this panel exactly. Never treat a last-decimal difference between two pages as an error without checking the rounding first.

How do I convert a price per tonne or per pound to price per kg?

Divide a per-metric-tonne quote by 1000: 850 per tonne = 0.85 per kg; 1,200 per tonne = 1.20 per kg. Divide a per-US-short-ton quote by 907.185: 1,100 per short ton = 1.21254 per kg (enter 1.21). Multiply a per-pound quote by 2.20462: 0.55 per lb = 1.21254 per kg. The conversions section works all three directions with the defaults rate, and the imperial example prices a 2000 lb order end to end.

How much steel can my budget buy?

The panel has no reverse mode, so work it backwards by hand: neat weight = budget ÷ ((1 + waste) × rate). At an example 1.20 per kg with 5% waste, a budget of 1000 buys 793.65 kg of neat steel (1000 ÷ 1.26), 5000 buys 3,968.25 kg, and 10000 buys 7,936.51 kg. (Derived arithmetic, not a panel output — the panel only runs weight → cost.) Remember the result is neat weight: the ordered weight is 5% higher, and the bars still have to fit stock lengths.

Should I add laps on top of the waste percentage?

Only if your takeoff does not already include them. Two consistent ways exist, and mixing them is the error: either take off neat straight lengths and use a combined allowance (published takeoff guides use 10% for laps and waste together), or take off explicit laps from the lap calculator and hooks from the hook calculator and then apply waste of 3–5% for cutting and handling only. Applying 10% on top of explicit laps pays for the laps twice — on a 10-tonne order that is a full tonne of phantom steel.

Why do two suppliers' quotes differ for the same weight?

Usually the basis differs, not the arithmetic. Check, in order: (1) the rate basis — per kg, per metric tonne, or per US short ton (a short-ton quote looks ~10% cheaper than the same rate per metric tonne); (2) whether waste is included in the rate or added separately; (3) theoretical vs actual (weighed) weight — delivered bars vary within tolerance, and the two bases never agree to the kilogram; (4) whether delivery, cutting/bending and tax are in or out; (5) whether one rate covers all diameters or each size prices separately; (6) the quote's date — steel prices move, and an old quote is not a price. Convert every quote to the same basis (per kg, waste stated, extras listed) before comparing.

Can I price a whole project with one rate?

Only as a first estimate. The panel takes a single rate for all steel, but suppliers often price diameters differently, and a project mixes sizes, grades and members. For a defensible figure: take off each member on its own tool (slab, footing, mat, column, beam, wall, pool, driveway), price each weight here at its own rate if rates differ, add the detailing extras (chairs, tie wire, hooks, laps), then add concrete on the concrete estimator and labour, delivery and tax by hand. One rate × one weight is a budget; the member-by-member build is a quote.
