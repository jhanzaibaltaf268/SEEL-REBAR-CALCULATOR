Rebar Chair / Bar Support Calculator

Work out how many bar chairs you need to support a top mat. Enter the slab size and the chair grid spacing, and get the total number of chairs and the chairs-per-m² density.

How to use it

  1. Enter the slab length and width in metres.
  2. Set the chair grid spacing in metres — 1.0 m is the calculator default, and 1.0–1.2 m is common for slab top mats.
  3. Read the total chairs, the rows × columns grid, the slab area and the chairs-per-m² density.
  4. If the drawing sets chairs back from the edges, enter the reduced size for the count — see the setback section below.
  5. Add spares and round up — the calculator gives the neat grid count, not the order quantity.

Formula

Chairs = (floor(L ÷ spacing) + 1) × (floor(W ÷ spacing) + 1).

Questions

How many rebar chairs do I need?

It depends on the slab size and the grid spacing, not just the area — the calculator gives the exact grid count. As a rough guide, about one chair per square metre at 1 m spacing works for large slabs, but on the calculator defaults (5 × 4 m at 1 m spacing) the grid gives 30 chairs, which is 1.50 per m², not 1.00. The small-slab difference is the edge rows, explained below.

What spacing for bar chairs?

Commonly 1.0–1.2 m each way for slab top mats, 1–1.5 m in general site practice, and 0.8 m for heavy reinforcement. Australian practice caps spacing at 600 mm for residential pavements and 800 mm for buildings. There is no universal code value — the drawing governs, and heavier mats need closer spacing so the top steel does not sag during the pour.

How does the calculator count the chairs?

It lays a grid over the slab and counts the grid lines, including both edges: chairs along the length = floor(length ÷ spacing) + 1, chairs along the width = floor(width ÷ spacing) + 1, and the total is the two multiplied. On the defaults that is (5 ÷ 1 + 1) × (4 ÷ 1 + 1) = 6 × 5 = 30 chairs. The +1 in each direction is the edge line — without it you would miss a whole row and column.

Why does the calculator give more chairs than one per square metre?

Because the grid includes edge rows, the count is always higher than area × density — the gap is just biggest on small slabs. A 2 × 2 m slab at 1 m spacing needs 9 chairs (2.25 per m²), a 5 × 4 m slab needs 30 (1.50 per m²), and even a 20 × 10 m slab needs 231 (1.16 per m²) against the 200 the area rule predicts. As slabs grow, the density tends towards 1 ÷ spacing² — 1.00 per m² at 1 m spacing — but it never quite reaches it.

What if the chairs are set back from the edges?

Then the calculator's edge-to-edge count overstates the order. On a 10 × 10 m slab at 1 m spacing the calculator gives 11 × 11 = 121 chairs, but with chairs set back 0.5 m from each edge there are only 10 positions per direction, giving 10 × 10 = 100 chairs. The fix: enter the reduced size (10 − 2 × 0.5 = 9 m each way) and the calculator gives 100 — but divide by the real 100 m² slab area for the true density (1.00 per m²), not the 1.23 per m² it shows against the reduced 81 m².

Individual chairs or continuous chair bars — which does this count?

Individual chairs on a grid only. If the design uses continuous chair bars or support lines, count those by row length from the approved layout instead — a row is not a grid of individual chairs, and forcing it into this calculator gives the wrong number. The same applies to bent chair bars, which are a different product (see the next answer).

Does the calculator work out bent chair-bar cutting lengths?

No — that is a different calculation, and this page does not pretend otherwise. A bent chair bar in a footing is cut as 2H + E + 2L − 8d (two heights, one head, two legs, four 90° bends at 2d each). One published footing example works out at 2292 mm: height 594 mm, head 400 mm, legs 400 mm, 12 mm bar. Take the cutting length from the bar bending schedule or drawing, not from this count calculator.

What height and type of chair do I need?

Height comes from the reinforcement section detail — the elevations of the bars being supported and where the chair bears — not from a generic subtraction. Types run from plastic clip chairs and precast supports to bent-bar chairs. Two published bar ranges look contradictory but are not: light slab chairs are commonly fabricated from 8–10 mm bars, while heavy cage chair bars are usually 12 mm and above (12/16/20 mm), because a thin chair bar buckles under a loaded cage. Match the product to the load, and confirm it against the drawing.

How many spare chairs should I order?

Add about 5% and always round UP — you cannot buy half a chair, and rounding down loses one. The defaults order becomes 30 × 1.05 = 31.5 → 32 chairs; the 10 × 8 m example becomes 99 × 1.05 = 103.95 → 104 chairs. Spares cover chairs crushed or knocked over during fixing and the pour.

Does the calculator include cost or weight?

No — it gives the neat grid count only. Price it as count × your unit price: 30 chairs at an example €2.00 each is €60.00 before spares, and 99 chairs is €198.00. As a weight sense-check only, published guide figures put one steel chair at roughly 0.15–0.25 kg, so 30 steel chairs weigh about 4.5–7.5 kg.
