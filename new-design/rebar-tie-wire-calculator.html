Rebar Tie Wire Calculator

Estimate the binding wire for a reinforcement grid. Enter the slab size, bar spacing, the percentage of intersections tied and the wire used per tie to get the total length and weight.

How to use it

  1. Enter the slab length and width in metres.
  2. Set the bar spacing in mm — 150 mm is the calculator default.
  3. Set the wire used per tie in mm — 300 mm is the default, and 250–350 mm covers most hand ties.
  4. Set the percentage of intersections tied — 100% ties everything; the drawing often calls for every edge intersection and about 50% in the field.
  5. Read the total wire length and weight, plus the intersection and tie counts — then add waste, since the calculator gives the neat grid total, not the order quantity.

Formula

Intersections = nx × ny. Ties = intersections × ratio. Wire length = ties × wire-per-tie; weight uses ~1.6 mm wire.

Questions

How much tie wire do I need for rebar?

Allow roughly 250–350 mm of wire per tie, and let the calculator total it from your grid. On the defaults (5 × 4 m slab, 150 mm spacing, 300 mm per tie, 100% tied) that is 918 ties × 300 mm = 275.40 m of wire, weighing 4.34 kg at the calculator's 1.6 mm wire weight. Change any input and the total follows the same chain: grid → ties → length → weight.

Do you tie every rebar intersection?

Often every intersection on the edges and about 50% in the field — set the percentage to match the drawing. On the defaults, 100% tying gives 918 ties, 275.40 m and 4.34 kg, while 50% gives 459 ties, 137.70 m and 2.17 kg: half the intersections, half the wire. The original FAQ answer said the same in one line; the numbers are what make it usable.

How much wire does one tie use?

It depends on the bar size: bigger bars need longer wraps. One published wrap formula (four wraps plus a 16 cm twisting allowance) gives 261 mm on an 8 mm bar, 286 mm on 10 mm, 311 mm on 12 mm, 361 mm on 16 mm and 411 mm on 20 mm. The calculator's 300 mm default sits between the 10 mm and 12 mm values, so it is a sensible middle default — but on 20 mm bars it undercounts by 111 mm per tie, which is 101.90 m over 918 ties.

Why does the calculator assume 1.6 mm wire?

Because 1.6 mm annealed wire is the common site choice, and its weight follows the same d²/162.28 rule as rebar: 1.6² ÷ 162.28 = 0.0157752 kg/m, or 63.39 metres per kilogram. Thinner wire gives more metres per kilogram (1.2 mm → 112.69 m/kg) and thicker wire fewer (2.0 mm → 40.57 m/kg) — the full table is below. Coating (annealed vs galvanised) does not change the weight at the same diameter; it changes corrosion resistance, not the estimate.

How does this compare with the 9–13 kg of wire per tonne of steel rule?

The rule is a thumb rule, not a code clause — one of its own sources admits IS code does not specify a quantity. On the defaults slab (232.57 kg of steel from the slab calculator) the wire is 4.34 kg at 100% tying, which is 18.7 kg per tonne — above the rule — but 2.17 kg at 50% tying, which is 9.3 kg per tonne, inside it. The rule assumes partial tying and mixed members; a dense slab grid tied at 100% genuinely uses more. Use the rule as a sanity check on a whole project, and the grid count for the actual order.

How many rolls or coils do I need?

Divide the length by the coil's metres per kilogram. At the calculator's 1.6 mm wire (63.39 m/kg), a 5 kg coil holds 316.95 m — enough for the defaults' 275.40 m with room to spare — and a 25 kg coil holds 1584.77 m. The large-slab example below needs 2693.40 m, or 42.49 kg, so two 25 kg rolls cover it. Always check the coil label: metres per kilogram is a property of the wire diameter, not of the brand.

How much waste should I add?

One published calculator recommends a 5–15% reserve for cutting, over-twisting, re-tying and handling losses. On the defaults that turns 4.34 kg into 4.56 kg at +5%, 4.78 kg at +10% and 5.00 kg at +15%. The calculator gives the neat grid total with no reserve, so add it before ordering — a coil short on the day of the pour costs more than the 5% ever will.

What about double ties, or ties per crossing?

The calculator counts one tie per tied intersection. A double tie (two wraps at one crossing) doubles the length for that crossing by hand: the defaults' 275.40 m becomes 550.80 m if every crossing is double-tied. One published calculator suggests planning about 1.5 ties per crossing to cover re-ties after bars shift during the pour — treat that as a planning allowance on top of the count, not as a second tie the calculator should have counted.

Does tie wire carry any structural load?

No — it only holds the bars in position while concrete is placed and cured. The concrete and the rebar carry the load; the wire stops the cage shifting, sagging or spreading during the pour and vibration. That is also why it ties chairs, spacer bars and laps, not just crossings: anything that must stay put until the concrete sets gets wired.

Does the calculator include laps, chairs or cost?

No — it gives the neat grid wire total only. Laps come from the lap length calculator (lapped bars need extra ties at the overlap), chairs from the chair calculator, the full takeoff from the quantity & takeoff calculator, and pricing from the cost calculator. The steel tonnage for the per-tonne check comes from the tonnage calculator.
