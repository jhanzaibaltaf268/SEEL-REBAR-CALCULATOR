How to use: Rebar Hook, Bend & Cutting Length Calculator

  1. Enter the straight (out-to-out) length in mm — the length after cover is taken off the span, not the full span itself.
  2. Pick the bar size.
  3. Enter the number of hooks (9d each) and the count of each bend type — 45°, 90°, 135°.
  4. Read the cutting length in mm and m, plus the hook addition and bend deduction shown separately.
  5. Apply the result to the bar schedule: divide the 12 m stock length by the cutting length for bars per stick, and add laps and waste before ordering.

Formula

Cutting length = straight length + (hooks × 9d) − (45°×1d + 90°×2d + 135°×3d).

The formula, explained

Cutting length is the length of straight bar you cut before bending — not the length shown on the drawing. The drawing gives out-to-out dimensions, which measure along the outside faces of the bent bar. Bending stretches the outside and compresses the inside, so the outside measurement always overstates the bar. The formula corrects both ends of the problem:

Cutting length = straight length + (hooks × 9d) − (45°×1d + 90°×2d + 135°×3d)

  • Straight length is the out-to-out length you enter — after cover is taken off the span. The calculator does not subtract cover for you.
  • Hooks × 9d is the extra steel at the bar ends. A standard 180° hook adds 9 × bar diameter per hook — the bent end plus its straight tail.
  • Bend deductions take back the stretch at each change of direction: 1d for 45°, 2d for 90°, 3d for 135°. Without them every bent bar comes out too long.

One trap to avoid before the examples: a 180° end is a hook in this calculator, so it takes the 9d addition. Some schedules list a 4d deduction for a 180° bend instead — that is the same end measured the other way. Never apply both the 9d addition and a 180° deduction to one end.

Worked example 1: calculator defaults (12 mm, 2 hooks)

Open the calculator and it already shows this case: 3000 mm straight length, 12 mm bar, 2 hooks (one each end), no bends.

  • Hook addition = 2 × 9 × 12 = 216 mm.
  • Bend deduction = 0 mm.
  • Cutting length = 3000 + 216 − 0 = 3216 mm.
  • Weight per bar = 3.216 × 0.887 = 2.85 kg.

Try it yourself: the panel opens on 3,216 mm, hook addition 216.00 mm, deduction 0.000 mm — and 3.22 m, because the panel rounds metres to 2 decimals (the exact value is 3.216 m).

Worked example 2: 16 mm bar with two 90° bends

Straight length 4500 mm, 16 mm bar, 2 hooks, two 90° bends:

  • Hook addition = 2 × 9 × 16 = 288 mm.
  • Bend deduction = 2 × 2 × 16 = 64 mm.
  • Cutting length = 4500 + 288 − 64 = 4724 mm.
  • Weight per bar = 4.724 × 1.578 = 7.45 kg.

Try it yourself: enter 4500, 16 mm, 2 hooks, two 90° bends and the panel shows 4,724 mm (4.72 m rounded). Note how the bends pull the result back: without them the same bar would cut at 4788 mm.

Worked example 3: mixed bends, one hook

Straight length 2500 mm, 10 mm bar, 1 hook, one 135° bend and one 45° bend:

  • Hook addition = 1 × 9 × 10 = 90 mm.
  • Bend deduction = 1 × 3 × 10 + 1 × 1 × 10 = 40 mm.
  • Cutting length = 2500 + 90 − 40 = 2550 mm.
  • Weight per bar = 2.55 × 0.616 = 1.57 kg.

Try it yourself: the panel shows 2,550 mm (2.55 m). And a deduction-only check, for a bar with bends but no hooks: 3000 mm, 12 mm, two 90° bends gives 3000 − 48 = 2952 mm — bends alone always shorten the cut.

Two hooks — the usual case, one each end — double these figures: a 12 mm bar with two hooks gains 216 mm, a 16 mm bar gains 288 mm. The calculator multiplies the hook count you enter, so a single-hook bar (an L-bar into a support, for example) takes just one allowance.

These are the practical site values used in most bar schedules (IS 2502 practice). The deduction grows with angle because a sharper bend stretches more steel around the outside. Count every bend in the bar — a U-bar has two 90° bends (4d total), a cranked bar has four 45° bends (4d total, see below).

Hook allowance vs anchorage value

The 9d figure above is a cutting-length addition. It is not the same as a hook's anchorage value — how much of the development length Ld a bend supplies. IS 456 Cl 26.2.2.1 gives anchorage values of 4d for a 45° bend, 8d for 90°, 12d for 135°, and 16d for a U-bend. Confusing the two is a real site error: a 90° hook does not "give you 9d of anchorage", it gives 8d.

The bridge between the two, using the development-length page's own figures: a 12 mm Fe500 bar in M25 concrete needs Ld = 582.59 mm in tension. A 90° hook supplies 8 × 12 = 96 mm of anchorage, so the straight length still needed is 582.59 − 96 = 486.59 mm. Use the development length calculator for Ld itself — this page only sizes the cut, not the embedment.

Stirrup hooks: why 10d, not 9d

Closed stirrups and column ties do not use the 9d hook. Their 135° seismic hooks take 10d each (IS 13920 practice: the hook must stay closed when concrete spalls in an earthquake, so the tail is longer and the bend is 135°, never 90°). That is why the site has a separate stirrup calculator — its formula is 2(a + b) + 2 × 10d − 3 × 2d − 2 × 3d, with three 90° bends and two 135° bends.

Worked with the stirrup formula, independent of the calculator defaults: inner dimensions 200 × 400 mm, 8 mm bar. Perimeter = 2 × (200 + 400) = 1200 mm. Hooks = 2 × 10 × 8 = 160 mm. Deductions = 3 × 2 × 8 + 2 × 3 × 8 = 96 mm. Cutting length = 1200 + 160 − 96 = 1264 mm. If you ran the same tie through this page's 9d hooks you would undercut it by 16 mm — small per tie, systematic across a column.

Cranked (bent-up) bars: the 0.42h rule

A bent-up bar rises through the slab on a 45° incline, and the incline is longer than the straight run it replaces. The standard site allowance is 0.42 × h per crank, where h is the clear crank height: slab depth − top cover − bottom cover − bar diameter. With two cranks per bar, add 2 × 0.42h — then deduct the four 45° bends (4 × 1d).

Worked: 150 mm slab, 25 mm covers, 12 mm bar. h = 150 − 25 − 25 − 12 = 88 mm. Extra per crank = 0.42 × 88 = 36.96 mm; two cranks = 73.92 mm. Less four 45° deductions (48 mm), the net addition over the straight length is 25.92 mm. This calculator has no crank input — add the net figure to its result yourself, and do not also count the crank bends in the bend inputs, or the deductions will be taken twice.

L-bars, U-bars and the bar schedule

Most scheduled bars are one of three shapes, and each is just the same formula with the bends counted:

Shape (BS 8666)Cutting-length recipeExample
00 — straight barStraight + hooks × 9dEx1: 3000 + 216 = 3216 mm
21 — L-barA + B − 2d (one 90° bend)500 + 800 − 24 = 1276 mm (12 mm); 1.13 kg
11 — U-barA + 2B − 4d (two 90° bends)400 + 2 × 600 − 48 = 1552 mm (12 mm)

The L-bar example is the standard textbook case: A 500 mm, B 800 mm, 12 mm bar, one 90° bend → 1300 − 24 = 1276 mm = 1.276 m, and at 0.887 kg/m that is 1.13 kg per bar (20 bars = 25.52 m). A bar schedule summarises by diameter, because steel is bought and paid by weight per diameter — so carry the cutting length, the count, and the total length per diameter into the schedule, not just the length.

From cutting length to order: stock lengths and waste

Bars arrive in 12 m stock lengths, so the cutting length decides how many bars each stick yields — and how much offcut you keep:

Cutting lengthBars per 12 m stickOffcut per stick
3216 mm (Ex1)312000 − 3 × 3216 = 2352 mm
4724 mm (Ex2)212000 − 2 × 4724 = 2552 mm

Plan the offcuts: short lengths become chairs, spacers and dowels instead of scrap. As a site norm, optimised cutting with off-cut reuse runs 2.0–2.5% waste, a normal site with a good schedule 2.5–4.0%, and cutting straight from drawings with no schedule 5–8% or more. Order the neat total plus the waste allowance — the calculator gives the neat cutting length only.

Metric and imperial units

This calculator takes millimetres. If your drawings use inches:

One cross-code warning: ACI detailing gives a 90° end extension of 12d (152.4 mm = 6.00 in on a 12.7 mm bar), not the 9d standard hook used here. The 9d rule is the IS/site convention this calculator follows — do not mix the two on one schedule. Convert first, then enter metric values.

What the calculator does not include

  • Cover subtraction. Enter the out-to-out length after cover is taken off the span. A full span entered raw overstates every bar.
  • Crank allowance. The 0.42h rule above is a hand addition — there is no crank input, and crank bends must not also be entered as bend inputs.
  • Laps. Splice lengths sit on top of cutting lengths. Size them with the lap length calculator.
  • Development length. Hooks supply anchorage value, but Ld itself comes from the development length calculator.
  • Closed stirrups and ties. These use 10d seismic hooks, not 9d — use the stirrup calculator.
  • Waste and offcuts. The result is the neat cutting length. Add the waste allowance (2.5–4% with a good schedule) before ordering.
  • ACI hook geometry. ACI standard hooks use a bend plus a 12d extension, a different shape from the 9d hook modelled here.
  • Bend radius / curved-portion geometry. This calculator uses the 9d/1d/2d/3d site rules, not a radius-based arc calculation — a radius-based hook-extension tool may give a different figure for the same hook.

Common mistakes

  1. Adding a 9d hook and a 180° deduction to the same end. A 180° end is one thing measured two ways. Pick the hook (9d added) and never also deduct 4d for it.
  2. Forgetting bend deductions. A bar cut to outside dimensions with no deductions comes out long at every bend — it will not fit the cage, and site trimming wastes the bar.
  3. Using 9d hooks on stirrups. Closed ties need 135° seismic hooks at 10d. The 9d rule undercuts every tie by 2d and, worse, a 90° tie hook can open when concrete spalls.
  4. Entering the full span instead of the length after cover. The calculator takes the straight length as given. Cover comes off before you type, on both ends.
  5. Treating the hook allowance as anchorage. 9d is what you add when cutting; 8d is what a 90° hook supplies toward Ld. Using 9d as if it satisfied development length overstates the anchorage by 1d per hook — and misses the point that Ld is checked separately.

Rebar Hook, Bend & Cutting Length Calculator — FAQ

What is the hook length of rebar?

A standard 180° hook adds 9 × bar diameter to the cutting length: 72 mm for 8 mm, 90 mm for 10 mm, 108 mm for 12 mm, 144 mm for 16 mm, 180 mm for 20 mm. Two hooks (one each end) double it. Note this is the cutting addition — the anchorage value a hook supplies toward development length is a different figure (8d for a 90° hook).

Why do we deduct for bends?

Bending stretches the outside of the bar and compresses the inside, so a bar measured along its outside faces looks longer than the steel actually is. The deduction takes that stretch back: 1d per 45° bend, 2d per 90° bend, 3d per 135° bend. Skip it and every bent bar comes out too long.

What is the cutting length formula?

Cutting length = straight length + (hooks × 9d) − (45°×1d + 90°×2d + 135°×3d). The calculator defaults (3000 mm, 12 mm, 2 hooks, no bends) give 3000 + 216 − 0 = 3216 mm.

What is the difference between a hook and a bend?

A hook is an end anchorage: a 180° turn at the bar end that adds 9d to the cut. A bend is a direction change along the bar that only takes a deduction (1d–3d by angle) and adds nothing. A 180° end counts as a hook — never add the 9d and also deduct for it.

How much is a 135° stirrup hook?

10 × bar diameter per hook, under seismic detailing practice (IS 13920) — not the 9d of this calculator. Closed ties need 135° hooks so they stay shut when concrete spalls. Use the stirrup calculator for closed ties; this page's 9d is for standard end hooks.

Does a hook count toward development length?

Yes, through its anchorage value — not through the 9d cutting addition. IS 456 gives 4d for 45°, 8d for 90°, 12d for 135° and 16d for a U-bend. Example: a 12 mm Fe500 bar in M25 concrete needs Ld = 582.59 mm, and a 90° hook supplies 96 mm of it, leaving 486.59 mm straight. Get Ld from the development length calculator.

How do you calculate an L-bar cutting length?

Add the two legs and deduct one 90° bend: A + B − 2d. For A 500 mm, B 800 mm, 12 mm bar: 500 + 800 − 24 = 1276 mm (1.276 m), weighing 1.13 kg at 0.887 kg/m.

How do you calculate a cranked (bent-up) bar?

Add 0.42 × h per 45° crank, where h = slab depth − top cover − bottom cover − bar diameter, then deduct the four 45° bends (4d). Example: 150 mm slab, 25 mm covers, 12 mm bar → h = 88 mm, two cranks add 73.92 mm, less 48 mm deductions = 25.92 mm net over the straight length. This calculator has no crank input — add it by hand and do not also enter the crank bends.

How many cut bars come from one 12 m stock length?

Divide 12000 mm by the cutting length and round down. The 3216 mm default bar gives 3 per stick (2352 mm offcut); the 4724 mm example gives 2 per stick (2552 mm offcut). Keep offcuts for chairs, spacers and dowels, and add 2.5–4% waste on top of the neat total.

What does this calculator not do?

It does not subtract concrete cover (enter the length after cover), does not add laps (use the lap calculator), does not model cranks or ACI hook geometry, and does not use the 10d seismic hook — closed ties belong in the stirrup calculator.
