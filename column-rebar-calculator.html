How to use: Column Rebar Calculator

  1. Enter the column height in metres and the cross-section in mm.
  2. Set the number and size of vertical bars, plus the lap/extra length per bar in metres.
  3. Set the tie spacing in mm and the tie bar size.
  4. Read the total weight, split into vertical steel and tie steel, plus the tie count and cut length.
  5. Multiply by the number of identical columns for the floor total, then add waste — the calculator gives one column, not the order quantity.

Formula

Vertical steel = bars × (height + lap) × kg/m. Ties = floor(height ÷ spacing) + 1, each at the stirrup cutting length.

The formula, explained

The formula has two halves — the vertical cage and the ties that hold it together — and the calculator keeps them separate so you can see which half dominates:

Vertical steel = bars × (height + lap) × kg/m; Ties = floor(height ÷ spacing) + 1, each at the stirrup cutting length; Total = vertical + tie steel

Each vertical bar runs the column height plus the lap/extra length you set — one extra length per bar, not one per column. Each tie is a closed stirrup whose cutting length comes from the inner dimensions: a = width − 2 × cover, b = depth − 2 × cover, cut = 2(a + b) + 20d − 6d − 6d, exactly as on the stirrup page. The tie count uses the same fence-post rule as every grid on this site: floor(height ÷ spacing) intervals need floor(height ÷ spacing) + 1 ties, because both end ties count.

One display note, carried over from the stirrup page: the tie-bar field reads "0.395|8" but the panel computes with the option value 0.394 kg/m, so every tie weight below uses 0.394. The vertical-bar default "1.578|16" matches its option exactly, so the vertical weights need no such adjustment.

Worked example 1: defaults (3 m, 300 × 450 mm)

Open the calculator and leave the defaults: height 3 m, section 300 × 450 mm, cover 40 mm, 6 vertical bars of 16 mm, lap 0.6 m, tie spacing 150 mm, tie bars 8 mm.

Vertical steel = 6 × (3 + 0.6) × 1.578 = 34.08 kg. Ties = floor(3000 ÷ 150) + 1 = 21. Tie cut: a = 300 − 80 = 220 mm, b = 450 − 80 = 370 mm, so 2 × (220 + 370) + 160 − 96 = 1244 mm — the exact tie the stirrup page works for this section at column cover, so the two pages agree. Tie steel = 21 × 1.244 × 0.394 = 10.29 kg.

Total = 34.08 + 10.29 = 44.38 kg (0.044 t). The panel shows 44.38 kg, 34.08 kg vertical, 10.29 kg ties, 21.00 ties, 1,244 mm cut and 0.044 t. Vertical steel is 76.8% of the total here — on most columns the verticals dominate and the ties are roughly a fifth to a quarter, which is a useful smell test: if your ties ever outweigh your verticals, check your spacing input.

Worked example 2: 400 × 400 mm column

Set height 3.6 m, section 400 × 400 mm, cover 40 mm, 8 vertical bars of 20 mm, lap 0.8 m, tie spacing 200 mm, tie bars 10 mm.

Vertical steel = 8 × (3.6 + 0.8) × 2.465 = 86.77 kg. Ties = floor(3600 ÷ 200) + 1 = 19. Tie cut: a = b = 400 − 80 = 320 mm, so 2 × 640 + 200 − 120 = 1360 mm. Tie steel = 19 × 1.36 × 0.616 = 15.92 kg.

Total = 86.77 + 15.92 = 102.69 kg (0.103 t). The steel percentage is 8 × 314.16 ÷ 160000 = 1.57% — comfortably inside the 0.8–6% band. The code-maximum tie spacing here is min(400, 16 × 20 = 320, 300) = 300 mm, so the 200 mm spacing is inside the limit with room for the tighter joint and lap zones the drawing may call for.

Worked example 3: small square column — and a failed check

Set height 3 m, section 300 × 300 mm, cover 40 mm, 4 vertical bars of 12 mm, lap 0.5 m, tie spacing 150 mm, tie bars 8 mm.

Vertical steel = 4 × (3 + 0.5) × 0.887 = 12.42 kg. Ties = 21. Tie cut: a = b = 300 − 80 = 220 mm, so 2 × 440 + 160 − 96 = 944 mm — again the stirrup page's own square-column tie. Tie steel = 21 × 0.944 × 0.394 = 7.81 kg.

Total = 12.42 + 7.81 = 20.23 kg (0.020 t). But the total is not the whole story: the steel area is 4 × 113.10 = 452.4 mm² against a gross area of 90000 mm², which is 0.50% — BELOW the IS 456 minimum of 0.8% (720 mm²). This cage fails the minimum even though the calculator totals it happily. The fix is one bar size up: 4 × 16 mm gives 804.2 mm², which is 0.89% and passes. A calculator that only totals weight would let this through silently; the percentage check is what catches it.

Ties by spacing

Same defaults column (3 m, 300 × 450 mm, 8 mm ties at 1244 mm each), five spacings. The vertical steel never moves — 34.08 kg in every row — so the whole table is the tie half doing its thing.

Two readings. First, spacing is a real lever: tightening from 200 mm to 100 mm adds 15 ties and 7.35 kg (+18%) on one column. Second, the 300 mm row is a trap, not a saving: the code maximum for this section is min(300, 16 × 16 = 256, 300) = 256 mm, so 300 mm spacing violates the limit even though the calculator totals it. The calculator counts whatever spacing you enter; the limit is your job to respect. And remember the drawing zones ties separately at joints, laps and the middle — one spacing for the full height is an estimating simplification, not a detailing instruction.

The maximum is 6% of gross area (5400 mm² on a 300 × 300 mm section, 15000 mm² on a 500 × 500 mm section), with 4% often used as the working limit where bars lap, because lapped bars double up and the section congests. The 500 × 500 mm row is the tight one: 10 × 16 mm only just clears the minimum at 0.80%, so one bar fewer fails it — always work the percentage, never guess the count. And a caution from the competitor field: one published worked example (4 × 20 mm in 400 × 400 mm) gives 0.79%, just under the minimum — even published examples slip, which is why the check belongs on the calculator page itself.

The lap field, explained honestly

The lap/extra field is the most misunderstood input on this page, so here is exactly what it is: ONE extra length per vertical bar, in metres, that you type in. The calculator does not compute a code lap from it, and the 0.6 m default is an allowance, not a code value.

What the code lap actually is depends on bar size, steel grade, concrete grade and tension vs compression — that is the lap length calculator's job. For the defaults bars (16 mm, Fe500, M25, tension) it is 776.79 mm. Setting the field to 0.777 m instead of 0.6 m raises the defaults vertical steel from 34.08 kg to 35.76 kg — a 1.68 kg difference on one column, 16.80 kg across ten columns. Small per column, not small per floor.

There is a second method in the field worth knowing about: one published guide adds lap at BOTH ends (height + 2 × 40d). On the defaults that gives bar length 3 + 2 × 0.64 = 4.28 m and vertical steel 40.52 kg — 6.44 kg above the default-lap figure. If your column is lapped top and bottom, set the field to the sum of both laps rather than a single lap; if it is lapped at one end only, set the single lap. Either way, get the lap length itself from the lap calculator or the drawing, never from the 0.6 m default.

From one column to the floor order

The calculator gives one column. The order is that column, times the number of identical columns, plus waste.

Example: ten columns at the defaults give 10 × 44.38 = 443.78 kg. Add 5% waste for cutting and laps (one published range is 3–5%): 443.78 × 1.05 = 465.97 kg. At an example price of €1.20/kg that is €532.53 for ten columns. Twenty columns give 887.55 kg neat, before waste.

For scale, a published guide example reproduced through this calculator — a 5 m, 300 × 500 mm column with 12 × 16 mm bars, 0.6 m lap and 8 mm ties at 200 mm — gives 106.04 kg vertical plus 13.77 kg of ties (26 ties at 1344 mm) = 119.81 kg for one column, at a steel percentage of 1.61%. For the full takeoff across members, use the quantity & takeoff calculator, price it with the cost calculator, and check the steel tonnage with the tonnage calculator. Starter bars and dowels into the footing come from the footing calculator.

Imperial conversions

The calculator takes metres and millimetres, so convert first: 1 m = 3.28 ft, 1 kg = 2.20 lb, 1 in = 25.4 mm.

Worked conversions: the defaults total 44.38 kg = 97.84 lb (0.044 t); example 2 totals 102.69 kg = 226.38 lb (0.103 t). A 3 m column is 9.84 ft high, and a 300 × 450 mm section is 11.81 × 17.72 in. The formula is unit-free — bars × (height + lap) × kg/m works in feet and lb/ft if every input is imperial — but the calculator takes metric, so convert before entering. Tie spacing in inches: 150 mm = 5.91 in, 200 mm = 7.87 in.

What this calculator does not cover

  • Capacity design — axial load, bending moment, slenderness and buckling checks; the steel percentage passing 0.8% does not prove the section carries its load.
  • Circular and spiral columns — rectangular tied columns only; circular layouts (minimum 6 bars) and spiral detailing come from the drawing.
  • Beam–column joint confinement and seismic confinement lengths — IS 13920 zone detailing, including tighter tie spacing at joints and lap zones, is not computed; the calculator uses one spacing for the full height.
  • Footing starter bars and dowels — see the footing calculator.
  • Couplers — mechanical splices replace laps with their own lengths and locations from the approved system.
  • Chairs and binding wire — see the chair calculator and the binding wire calculator.
  • Cost and multi-column takeoff — the order section shows the hand arithmetic; the cost calculator prices a full takeoff and the quantity & takeoff calculator totals across members.

5 common mistakes

  1. Forgetting the +1 on ties. floor(height ÷ spacing) without the +1 gives 20 ties instead of 21 on the defaults — one 1244 mm tie, 0.49 kg, missing from every column. The end ties are real ties.
  2. Treating the 0.6 m lap default as the code lap. For 16 mm Fe500 M25 tension bars the code lap is 776.79 mm, not 600 mm — trusting the default understates the vertical steel by 1.68 kg per column, 16.80 kg across ten columns.
  3. Steel below the 0.8% minimum. 4 × 12 mm in a 300 × 300 mm column gives 0.50% and fails the minimum even though the calculator totals it without complaint. Always work the percentage; the fix here is 4 × 16 mm (0.89%).
  4. Spacing wider than the code maximum. 300 mm ties on the defaults look like a saving (39.48 kg vs 44.38 kg) but exceed the 256 mm maximum for that section. The calculator counts any spacing you enter — the limit is yours to respect.
  5. Cutting ties to outer dimensions. The tie cut comes from the inner dimensions (width and depth less twice the cover). Using the outer 300 × 450 mm section gives a 1500 mm perimeter instead of 1180 mm — 320 mm too long per tie, 6.72 m and 2.65 kg over on 21 ties — and the tie will not fit with its cover intact.

Column Rebar Calculator — FAQ

How is column rebar calculated?

Add the vertical steel to the tie steel. Vertical steel = number of bars × (height + lap) × kg/m; ties = floor(height ÷ spacing) + 1, each cut to the stirrup cutting length. On the calculator defaults (3 m, 300 × 450 mm, 6 × 16 mm bars, 0.6 m lap, 150 mm ties, 8 mm tie bars) that is 34.08 kg of vertical steel plus 10.29 kg of tie steel = 44.38 kg for one column.

How many vertical bars in a column?

At least 4 for a rectangular column and 6 for a circular column, subject to design — but the count alone is not the check. The steel area must also clear the 0.8% minimum of the gross section: the defaults (6 × 16 mm in 300 × 450 mm) give 0.89% and pass, while 4 × 12 mm in 300 × 300 mm gives only 0.50% and fails — that section needs 4 × 16 mm (0.89%) instead.

What is the minimum and maximum steel percentage in a column?

IS 456 sets longitudinal steel between 0.8% and 6% of the gross cross-sectional area. In practice 4% is often used as the working limit where bars lap, because lapped bars double up and the section congests. The percentage table below works the minimum for common sections; the maximum is rarely the binding constraint on ordinary columns.

What spacing do column ties need?

Maximum spacing = the smallest of the least lateral dimension, 16 × the smallest longitudinal bar diameter, and 300 mm. On the defaults (300 mm side, 16 mm bars) that is min(300, 256, 300) = 256 mm, so the calculator's 150 mm default is comfortably inside. Note the calculator uses one spacing for the full height, while drawings zone the ties separately at joints, laps and the middle — the drawing's zone spacings govern where they are tighter.

What size should column ties be?

At least the larger of 6 mm and one quarter of the largest longitudinal bar diameter. On the defaults (16 mm bars) that is 6 mm, so the 8 mm tie bars are fine; on 25 mm bars it is 6.25 mm, so 8 mm is the practical minimum. One published guide states 8 mm flat — the code floor is 6 mm, and 8 mm is the safer common choice.

What does the lap/extra field mean?

It is ONE extra length per vertical bar, in metres, that you set yourself — the calculator does not compute a code lap. The default 0.6 m is an allowance, not a code value: the code tension lap for a 16 mm Fe500 bar in M25 concrete is 776.79 mm, so setting 0.777 m instead of 0.6 m on the defaults raises the vertical steel from 34.08 kg to 35.76 kg (+1.68 kg). Use the lap length calculator for the code lap, and if the column is lapped top and bottom, set the field to the sum of both laps.

Does the calculator design the column?

No — it totals the steel for a cage you describe; it does not check whether the cage is adequate. Axial and moment capacity, slenderness and buckling, seismic confinement lengths and joint detailing all come from the structural design and the drawing. A total that looks reasonable is not proof the section works, and a section that passes the 0.8% minimum is not proof it carries the load.

How many ties does a column need?

Ties = floor(height ÷ spacing) + 1 — the +1 is the tie at the far end, and forgetting it is the classic off-by-one: on the defaults it gives 20 ties instead of 21, short by one 1244 mm tie (0.49 kg). The calculator counts 21 at 150 mm spacing on a 3 m column; at 100 mm it counts 31, and at 300 mm only 11 — but 300 mm exceeds the 256 mm code maximum for this section, so it is not a legitimate saving.

Can I use this calculator for circular or spiral columns?

No — it is rectangular tied columns only. Circular columns need at least 6 longitudinal bars and a different bar layout, and spiral columns use a continuous helix with its own pitch and detailing rules instead of individual closed ties. Take both from the drawing; the tie-cutting method on the stirrup calculator still gives the closed-tie length, but the count and layout are not this calculator's.

Does the calculator include waste, starter bars or cost?

No — it gives the neat one-column total only. Add 3–5% waste for cutting and laps (a published range), take footing starter bars and dowels from the footing calculator, price the order with the cost calculator, and check the steel tonnage with the tonnage calculator. For a full takeoff across members use the quantity & takeoff calculator.
