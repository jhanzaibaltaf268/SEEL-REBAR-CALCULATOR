How to use: Rebar Development Length Calculator

  1. Pick the bar size.
  2. Select the steel grade (Fe250–Fe550) and concrete grade (M20–M40).
  3. Choose tension or compression.
  4. Read Ld in mm, as a multiple of diameter, and in metres — plus the τbd used.
  5. Compare with the tables below before detailing the anchorage.

Formula

Ld = (φ × 0.87 × fy) ÷ (4 × τbd). τbd is the design bond stress (increased 60% for deformed bars, +25% for compression).

See the full rebar weight chart (kg/m & lb/ft) →

Why 0.87 × fy? The design stress in the bar is the yield strength divided by the partial safety factor for steel (1.15), and 1 ÷ 1.15 = 0.87. Why 4 × τbd? Bond acts around the bar's perimeter (π × φ) over the length Ld, while the bar force acts on its area (π × φ² ÷ 4) — equating the two gives Ld = φ × σs ÷ (4 × τbd). Why does Ld grow with diameter? A thicker bar carries force proportional to φ² but bonds only proportional to φ, so the length must grow with φ to develop the same stress.

Worked example 1: 12 mm, Fe500, M25 (defaults)

Bar 12 mm, Fe500 (fy = 500 N/mm²), M25 concrete, tension. These are the values the calculator opens with, so you can check the result immediately:

  1. Design stress: 0.87 × 500 = 435 N/mm².
  2. Bond stress for M25 deformed bars: 1.4 × 1.6 = 2.24 N/mm² (see the table below).
  3. Ld = (12 × 435) ÷ (4 × 2.24) = 5,220 ÷ 8.96 = 582.59 mm — that is 48.55 × the bar diameter, or 0.583 m.

Try it yourself: enter the same numbers in the calculator above and you will get the same result.

Worked example 2: 16 mm, Fe500, M25

Same grades, larger bar:

  1. Ld = (16 × 435) ÷ (4 × 2.24) = 6,960 ÷ 8.96 = 776.79 mm.
  2. As a multiple: 776.79 ÷ 16 = 48.55 × d — the same multiple as example 1, because the multiple does not depend on bar size (see below).

The length grows with the bar, but the ×d multiple stays fixed for a given steel and concrete grade. That is why engineers often quote Ld as "about 49 diameters" for Fe500 in M25.

Worked example 3: compression

Same bar and grades as example 1, but in compression:

  1. Bond stress rises 25%: 2.24 × 1.25 = 2.80 N/mm².
  2. Ld = (12 × 435) ÷ (4 × 2.80) = 5,220 ÷ 11.20 = 466.07 mm — that is 38.84 × d.

Compression needs only 80% of the tension length (1 ÷ 1.25), because bond is better when the bar is pushed rather than pulled. The calculator applies this automatically when you switch the stress type.

Two patterns to remember: stronger steel needs longer development (Fe500 > Fe415 at every grade), and stronger concrete needs shorter development (every row falls left to right). Compression multiples are 80% of these values.

Anchorage value of bends and hooks

When straight embedment will not fit, a bend or hook counts toward Ld (IS 456, clause 26.2.2.1):

Bend / hookAnchorage value
45° bend4 × d
90° bend8 × d
135° bend12 × d
U-hook (180°)16 × d

Example: a 12 mm bar needing 582.59 mm in tension can use a 90° bend worth 8 × 12 = 96 mm plus 486.59 mm of straight length. For bend and hook cutting lengths, use the hook calculator.

The two are related but not interchangeable: a lap must be at least the development length, but lap rules add their own conditions. For splice lengths, use the lap calculator.

What the calculator does not include

  • Lap splices. Ld anchors one bar; joining two bars is a lap — see the lap calculator.
  • Bend and hook cutting lengths. The anchorage values above are allowances, not cutting lengths — see the hook calculator.
  • Seismic and ductile detailing. Special confinement, hooks and anchorage rules for earthquake zones are beyond this tool.
  • ACI development length. This tool follows IS 456. The ACI method uses different factors (casting position, coating, bar size, concrete strength) and gives different lengths.
  • Reduced stress. Ld here assumes the bar develops its full design stress (0.87 × fy). A bar stressed below that needs proportionally less — the tool does not take a reduced stress input.

5 common mistakes

  1. Using plain-bar bond stress for deformed bars. That understates τbd and overstates Ld by 60% — or, if you copy a plain-bar example, understates the real requirement. This calculator already applies the 60% increase.
  2. Forgetting the compression factor — or applying it backwards. Compression raises τbd by 25%, so Ld gets shorter (× 0.8), not longer.
  3. Using fy instead of 0.87 × fy. The design stress is 0.87 × fy, not fy. Using fy overstates Ld by 15%.
  4. Confusing Ld with lap length. Ld anchors a bar into concrete; a lap joins two bars. Same order of size, different detail — do not substitute one for the other.
  5. Assuming the ×d multiple changes with bar size. It does not: for fixed grades, Ld ÷ d is constant. A 20 mm bar needs 48.55 × 20 = 971 mm in Fe500/M25 tension, not a different multiple.

Rebar Development Length Calculator — FAQ

What is development length of rebar?

The length of bar that must be embedded in concrete to transfer its design force safely. Without enough embedment, the bar slips or pulls out before reaching its design stress.

What is the development length for Fe500 in M25?

About 47–49 times the bar diameter in tension for deformed bars — exactly 48.55 × d with this calculator: 582.59 mm for a 12 mm bar, 776.79 mm for a 16 mm bar, both in M25 tension.

Is development length different in tension and compression?

Yes. Compression raises the bond stress by 25%, so Ld is 80% of the tension value: a 12 mm Fe500 bar in M25 needs 582.59 mm in tension but 466.07 mm in compression.

Why is the stress 0.87 × fy and not fy?

0.87 is 1 ÷ 1.15, the partial safety factor for steel in IS 456. The design stress is the yield strength reduced by that factor — using full fy would overstate Ld by 15%.

Do deformed bars need shorter development length than plain bars?

Yes — deformed bars get a 60% higher bond stress (for example M25: 2.24 instead of 1.4 N/mm²), so their Ld is proportionally shorter. This calculator already uses deformed values.

Can a bend or hook replace straight development length?

Partly. A 90° bend counts as 8 × d and a U-hook as 16 × d toward Ld, with the rest made up in straight length. For cutting lengths, use the hook calculator.

What is the difference between development length and lap length?

Development length anchors one bar into concrete; lap length overlaps two bars to transfer force between them. A lap must be at least the development length, but lap rules add their own conditions — see the lap calculator.

Does stronger concrete reduce development length?

Yes. Higher grades raise the bond stress, so Ld falls: Fe500 in tension needs 56.64 × d in M20 but only 35.77 × d in M40.

Does the ×d multiple change with bar size?

No. For fixed steel and concrete grades the multiple is constant — only the length in mm grows with diameter. Fe500 in M25 is 48.55 × d whether the bar is 12 mm or 20 mm.

Does this calculator follow ACI or IS 456?

IS 456. The ACI method uses different factors (casting position, epoxy coating, bar size, concrete strength) and gives different lengths — do not mix the two methods on one detail.
