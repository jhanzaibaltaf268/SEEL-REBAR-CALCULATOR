How to use: House Steel Calculator

  1. Enter the total built-up area of the house — all floors added together, not the plot size or one floor alone.
  2. Choose the area unit: sq ft or m². The calculator converts m² to sq ft (× 10.7639) before applying the rate.
  3. Pick the storeys: ground only, or ground plus one floor (G+1). Two-storey houses carry more steel per sq ft.
  4. Read the neat range in kg and tonnes, then the order quantity with 5% wastage added — the high end plus wastage is the number to order against.
  5. Use the result for budgeting only. The final quantity comes from your structural drawings and bar bending schedule.

Formula

Steel (kg) = built-up area (sq ft) × rate (kg/sq ft). Ground only: 3.5–4 kg/sq ft. Ground+1: 4–4.5 kg/sq ft. Order quantity = neat total × 1.05.

How the total splits across members

There is no fixed split: the share each member takes depends on spans, soil, storeys and layout. As a rough planning illustration only, one published house-steel guide allocates footings 20–25%, columns 15–20%, beams 15–20%, slab 25–30% and lintels, staircase and other members 5–10% of the total — and states plainly that these are approximate examples, not a schedule. Do not order by these shares. A separate rule gives steel as a percentage of concrete volume (slab 1%, beam 2%, column 2.5%, footing 0.8%); that rule needs concrete volumes per member, which an area-based estimate does not have.

Why totals differ between sites

Three choices move the answer: the rate (3–3.5 kg/sq ft for a simple house, 3.5–4.5 for a typical house, 4.5–5 for a demanding design), the area basis (built-up area across all floors, not carpet area and not one slab), and whether wastage is included (this page shows neat and +5% side by side). A site quoting 3 tonnes for 1000 sq ft is using about 3 kg/sq ft; a site quoting 4.5 tonnes is using 4.5 kg/sq ft. Neither is wrong as a band — they are different points in the range, and the design decides where a real house lands.

What this calculator does not do

It does not design anything. It gives no bar sizes, counts, spacings, laps or bending shapes, and it does not know your soil, spans, seismic zone or local code. It counts one house as a single area, so an L-shaped plan, a large cantilever, a basement or a retaining wall all fall outside it. For those, use the member calculators and the bar bending schedule from your structural drawings.

House Steel Calculator — FAQ

How much steel is required for a 1000 sq ft house?

A 1000 sq ft single-storey house needs about 3,500–4,000 kg (3.5–4 tonnes) of steel. The same area over ground plus one floor needs about 4,000–4,500 kg. Add 5% wastage and the order quantities become 3,675–4,200 kg for ground only and 4,200–4,725 kg for ground plus one floor.

How much steel is required for a 1500 sq ft house?

A 1500 sq ft single-storey house needs about 5,250–6,000 kg (5.25–6 tonnes). Over ground plus one floor it needs about 6,000–6,750 kg (6–6.75 tonnes). With 5% wastage the order quantities are 5,512.5–6,300 kg and 6,300–7,087.5 kg.

How much steel is required for a 2000 sq ft house?

A 2000 sq ft single-storey house needs about 7,000–8,000 kg (7–8 tonnes). As a two-storey (duplex) house it needs about 8,000–9,000 kg (8–9 tonnes). With 5% wastage the order quantities are 7,350–8,400 kg and 8,400–9,450 kg.

What kg per sq ft rate does the calculator use?

Ground only: 3.5–4 kg per sq ft of built-up area. Ground plus one floor: 4–4.5 kg per sq ft. These are the published residential thumb-rule bands; some guides quote 4.5–4.75 kg per sq ft for ground-plus-one houses and 5–5.5 kg per sq ft for commercial buildings, so a heavy or long-span design can run above this calculator's high end.

Does the total include wastage?

The neat range does not. The calculator shows the neat total and, separately, the order quantity with 5% added for cutting, bending, hooks and laps — the allowance published takeoff guides recommend. Laps on very long bar runs and a second reinforcement layer are extra; work those out on the lap calculator and the member tools.

How is the steel split across footing, column, beam and slab?

Only approximately. One published house-steel guide gives illustrative shares of the total: footings 20–25%, columns 15–20%, beams 15–20%, slab 25–30%, lintels, staircase and other members 5–10% — explicitly approximate examples, not a reinforcement schedule. A second, different basis is the concrete-volume rule (slab 1%, beam 2%, column 2.5%, footing 0.8% of concrete volume), which needs concrete volumes, not area. For member-by-member quantities use the footing calculator, column calculator, beam calculator and slab calculator.

Why do different sites give different totals for the same house?

Because they pick different rates and different areas. Published bands range from 3–3.5 kg per sq ft for a simple single-storey house to 4.5–5 kg per sq ft for a demanding design, and some pages multiply by carpet area or a single slab instead of total built-up area. This calculator states its rate and its area basis up front, so you can see exactly which choice moves the total.

Is this enough to order steel?

No — it is a budgeting estimate. Order from the structural drawings and the bar bending schedule, which lists every bar by mark, diameter, shape, length and weight. Use this page to sanity-check a supplier quote, then refine with the quantity calculator for area-based takeoffs and the cost calculator for price.
