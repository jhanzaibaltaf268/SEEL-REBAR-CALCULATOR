How to use: Rebar Chair / Bar Support Calculator

  1. Enter the slab length and width in metres.
  2. Set the chair grid spacing in metres — 1.0 m is the calculator default, and 1.0–1.2 m is common for slab top mats.
  3. Read the total chairs, the rows × columns grid, the slab area and the chairs-per-m² density.
  4. If the drawing sets chairs back from the edges, enter the reduced size for the count — see the setback section below.
  5. Add spares and round up — the calculator gives the neat grid count, not the order quantity.

Formula

Chairs = (floor(L ÷ spacing) + 1) × (floor(W ÷ spacing) + 1).

The formula, explained

The formula counts grid lines, not area — and the +1 in each direction is the whole point:

Chairs = (floor(L ÷ spacing) + 1) × (floor(W ÷ spacing) + 1)

Picture the chairs as dots on a grid drawn over the slab. Along the length, the dots sit at 0, spacing, 2 × spacing, and so on up to the far edge — that is floor(L ÷ spacing) intervals, but floor(L ÷ spacing) + 1 dots, because both end dots count. Same along the width. Multiply the two dot counts and you have every chair position. The formula assumes chairs stand on the slab edges themselves; if the drawing sets them back from the edges, use the setback adjustment below instead of the raw size.

That edge assumption is also why the "one chair per square metre" rule of thumb never quite matches: area × density counts the middle of the slab and forgets the edges, so it always undercounts. On a big slab the error shrinks; on a small slab it dominates.

Worked example 1: 5 × 4 m slab (calculator defaults)

Open the calculator and leave the defaults: length 5 m, width 4 m, spacing 1.0 m.

Along the length: floor(5 ÷ 1) + 1 = 6 chairs. Along the width: floor(4 ÷ 1) + 1 = 5 chairs.

Total = 6 × 5 = 30 chairs; area = 20 m²; density = 30 ÷ 20 = 1.50 chairs per m². The panel shows 30.00 chairs, a 6.00 (× 5) grid, 20.00 m², and 1.50 /m², with the note "(6 × 5) chairs @ 1m grid".

Note the density: 1.50 per m², not the 1.00 the rule of thumb promises. The area rule would order 20 chairs for this slab and leave you 10 short — a third of the grid missing.

Worked example 2: same count, bigger slab (6 × 5 m)

Set length 6 m, width 5 m, spacing 1.2 m.

Along the length: floor(6 ÷ 1.2) + 1 = 5 + 1 = 6 chairs. Along the width: floor(5 ÷ 1.2) + 1 = 4 + 1 = 5 chairs.

Total = 6 × 5 = 30 chairs; area = 30 m²; density = 1.00 per m². Same 30 chairs as example 1, on a slab half as big again. Chair count follows the spacing and the edge lines, not the area — doubling the area does not double the chairs unless the spacing stays put.

Worked example 3: 10 × 8 m slab

Set length 10 m, width 8 m, spacing 1.0 m.

Along the length: floor(10 ÷ 1) + 1 = 11 chairs. Along the width: floor(8 ÷ 1) + 1 = 9 chairs.

Total = 11 × 9 = 99 chairs; area = 80 m²; density = 99 ÷ 80 = 1.24 per m² (exact 1.2375). At an example €2.00 per chair that is €198.00 before spares — the order section below adds the 5% and rounds up to 104.

Chairs by spacing

Same slab (5 × 4 m), five spacings. Tightening the grid costs chairs fast at first, then in steps — because the count only changes when a whole extra grid line fits.

Two practical consequences. First, the dead bands: on this slab, 0.9 m spacing gives exactly the same 30 chairs as 1.0 m, and every spacing from just over 1.0 m up to 1.25 m gives 20 — tightening from 1.2 m to 1.1 m buys nothing here, so check the calculator before paying for a tighter grid. Second, the Australian maxima land hard on this slab: 0.6 m for residential pavements means 63 chairs (more than double the 1.0 m count), and 0.8 m for buildings means 42.

Why small slabs need more chairs per m²

At 1.0 m spacing, watch what happens as the slab grows:

SlabGridChairsAreaChairs per m²
2 × 2 m3 × 394 m²2.25
5 × 4 m6 × 53020 m²1.50
10 × 8 m11 × 99980 m²1.24
20 × 10 m21 × 11231200 m²1.16

The pattern is exact: as a slab gets large, density tends towards 1 ÷ spacing² — 1.00 per m² at 1 m spacing — because the edge rows become a smaller share of the total. On small slabs the edges dominate, which is why the "one per square metre" rule is most wrong exactly where a home builder uses it: it undercounts the 5 × 4 m slab by 50% (20 predicted, 30 needed) and even the 20 × 10 m slab by 15.5% (200 predicted, 231 needed). Use the rule as a floor for a rough guess, never as the order.

Set back from the edges? Adjust the size

The calculator puts chairs on the slab edges. If the drawing sets them back — a common detail, since chairs near formwork need cover too — the raw count overstates the order, and the gap is not small.

Take a 10 × 10 m slab at 1 m spacing. The calculator gives 11 × 11 = 121 chairs. With chairs set back 0.5 m from each edge, there are only 10 positions per direction (the available run is 10 − 2 × 0.5 = 9 m, giving 9 ÷ 1 + 1 = 10 positions), so the true count is 10 × 10 = 100 chairs — 21 fewer.

The fix takes ten seconds: enter the reduced size (10 − 2 × 0.5 = 9 m each way) and the calculator gives 100 chairs directly. One caution: the density it then shows (100 ÷ 81 = 1.23 per m²) is against the reduced 81 m², not the real slab — the true density is 100 ÷ 100 = 1.00 per m². Use the adjusted size for the count, the real size for the density.

Bent chair bars are a different count

Two products share the word "chair", and they are counted differently. Everything above counts individual supports on a grid. A bent chair bar — a single bar bent into a chair shape to hold an upper cage above a lower cage in a footing or raft — is cut to length, not counted on a grid, and this calculator does not cut it.

Its cutting length is: Cut = 2H + E + 2L − 8d — two heights, one head, two legs, minus four 90° bends at 2d each. One published footing example: depth 750 mm, covers 50 mm, main bars 20 mm, distribution bars 16 mm at 150 mm centres, chair bar 12 mm. Height H = 750 − (100 + 20 + 16 + 20) = 594 mm; head E = 2 × 150 + 2 × 50 = 400 mm; legs L = 2 × 150 + 2 × 50 = 400 mm; cut = 2 × 594 + 400 + 2 × 400 − 8 × 12 = 2292 mm. The head and legs should each cover three bars for a firm grip. Take the cutting length from the bar bending schedule or drawing — and if the design uses continuous chair bars or support lines, count those by row length from the approved layout, never as a grid of individual chairs.

From grid count to order

The calculator gives the neat grid count. The order is the count, plus spares, priced.

Example: the defaults give 30 chairs. At an example €2.00 per chair that is €60.00. Add 5% for chairs crushed or knocked over during fixing and the pour: 30 × 1.05 = 31.5, which rounds UP to 32 chairs — never down, since rounding down to 31 would leave the grid one chair short. The 10 × 8 m example: 99 × €2.00 = €198.00, and 99 × 1.05 = 103.95 → 104 chairs.

As a weight sense-check only (not a calculator output): published guide figures put one steel chair at roughly 0.15–0.25 kg, so 30 steel chairs weigh about 4.5–7.5 kg — useful when comparing steel chairs against plastic ones, which weigh far less but cost more per piece. For the full takeoff across members, use the quantity & takeoff calculator, price it with the cost calculator, and estimate the wire for tying chairs to bars with the binding wire calculator. The mat itself — bar count and weight — comes from the slab calculator.

Imperial conversions

The calculator takes metres, so convert first: 1 m = 3.28 ft, 1 m² = 10.76 sq ft. Common spacings: 0.6 m = 1.97 ft, 0.8 m = 2.62 ft, 1.0 m = 3.28 ft, 1.2 m = 3.94 ft.

Worked example: a 20 × 16 ft slab is 6.096 × 4.8768 m. At a 1.0 m grid: floor(6.096 ÷ 1) + 1 = 7 chairs along the length, floor(4.8768 ÷ 1) + 1 = 5 along the width — 7 × 5 = 35 chairs, 29.73 m², 1.18 per m².

Cross-check against published practice: a 1000 sq ft slab is 92.90 m², and one guide puts it at 150–200 chairs. A square 1000 sq ft slab (9.64 × 9.64 m) at 0.8 m spacing gives 13 × 13 = 169 chairs — inside that range, which is reassuring; at 1.0 m spacing it gives only 100, which shows how much the spacing assumption matters to any published range.

What this calculator does not cover

  • Chair height, type and material — height comes from the reinforcement section detail; the choice between plastic, steel and precast supports comes from the drawing and specification.
  • Bent chair-bar cutting lengths — the formula and one worked example are given above, but the cutting length comes from the bar bending schedule, not from this count.
  • Continuous chair bars and support lines — counted by row length from the approved layout, not as a grid of individual chairs.
  • Edge setback as a direct input — use the size adjustment in the setback section; the calculator itself counts edge to edge.
  • Cost and weight — the order section shows the hand arithmetic; the cost calculator prices a full takeoff.
  • The mat itself — bar count, laps and weight come from the slab calculator; tying wire from the binding wire calculator.
  • Rectangular grids with different spacings each way — the calculator takes one square grid spacing; for a rectangular grid, run each direction's count by hand from the formula.

5 common mistakes

  1. Ordering by area instead of by grid. "One chair per square metre" undercounts every slab — by 50% on a 5 × 4 m slab (20 ordered, 30 needed) and still by 15.5% on a 20 × 10 m slab. Count the grid lines, including the edges.
  2. Ignoring the edge setback. On a 10 × 10 m slab at 1 m spacing, edge-to-edge counting gives 121 chairs but a 0.5 m setback gives 100. Twenty-one extra chairs is a small cost, but the same error in reverse — ordering the setback count for an edge-to-edge layout — leaves a row missing.
  3. Counting continuous rows as individual chairs. A continuous chair bar is ordered by length, not by grid positions. Forcing it through this calculator invents chairs that do not exist and hides the length that does.
  4. Rounding spares down. 30 chairs plus 5% is 31.5 — order 32, not 31. A grid with one position unfilled is a top mat that sags at exactly that spot during the pour.
  5. One spacing for every slab. 1.0–1.2 m suits ordinary slab top mats, but residential pavements cap at 600 mm and buildings at 800 mm in Australian practice, and heavy mats need 0.8 m or tighter. The drawing's spacing governs — the calculator only counts the grid you give it.

Rebar Chair / Bar Support Calculator — FAQ

How many rebar chairs do I need?

It depends on the slab size and the grid spacing, not just the area — the calculator gives the exact grid count. As a rough guide, about one chair per square metre at 1 m spacing works for large slabs, but on the calculator defaults (5 × 4 m at 1 m spacing) the grid gives 30 chairs, which is 1.50 per m², not 1.00. The small-slab difference is the edge rows, explained below.

What spacing for bar chairs?

Commonly 1.0–1.2 m each way for slab top mats, 1–1.5 m in general site practice, and 0.8 m for heavy reinforcement. Australian practice caps spacing at 600 mm for residential pavements and 800 mm for buildings. There is no universal code value — the drawing governs, and heavier mats need closer spacing so the top steel does not sag during the pour.

How does the calculator count the chairs?

It lays a grid over the slab and counts the grid lines, including both edges: chairs along the length = floor(length ÷ spacing) + 1, chairs along the width = floor(width ÷ spacing) + 1, and the total is the two multiplied. On the defaults that is (5 ÷ 1 + 1) × (4 ÷ 1 + 1) = 6 × 5 = 30 chairs. The +1 in each direction is the edge line — without it you would miss a whole row and column.

Why does the calculator give more chairs than one per square metre?

Because the grid includes edge rows, the count is always higher than area × density — the gap is just biggest on small slabs. A 2 × 2 m slab at 1 m spacing needs 9 chairs (2.25 per m²), a 5 × 4 m slab needs 30 (1.50 per m²), and even a 20 × 10 m slab needs 231 (1.16 per m²) against the 200 the area rule predicts. As slabs grow, the density tends towards 1 ÷ spacing² — 1.00 per m² at 1 m spacing — but it never quite reaches it.

What if the chairs are set back from the edges?

Then the calculator's edge-to-edge count overstates the order. On a 10 × 10 m slab at 1 m spacing the calculator gives 11 × 11 = 121 chairs, but with chairs set back 0.5 m from each edge there are only 10 positions per direction, giving 10 × 10 = 100 chairs. The fix: enter the reduced size (10 − 2 × 0.5 = 9 m each way) and the calculator gives 100 — but divide by the real 100 m² slab area for the true density (1.00 per m²), not the 1.23 per m² it shows against the reduced 81 m².

Individual chairs or continuous chair bars — which does this count?

Individual chairs on a grid only. If the design uses continuous chair bars or support lines, count those by row length from the approved layout instead — a row is not a grid of individual chairs, and forcing it into this calculator gives the wrong number. The same applies to bent chair bars, which are a different product (see the next answer).

Does the calculator work out bent chair-bar cutting lengths?

No — that is a different calculation, and this page does not pretend otherwise. A bent chair bar in a footing is cut as 2H + E + 2L − 8d (two heights, one head, two legs, four 90° bends at 2d each). One published footing example works out at 2292 mm: height 594 mm, head 400 mm, legs 400 mm, 12 mm bar. Take the cutting length from the bar bending schedule or drawing, not from this count calculator.

What height and type of chair do I need?

Height comes from the reinforcement section detail — the elevations of the bars being supported and where the chair bears — not from a generic subtraction. Types run from plastic clip chairs and precast supports to bent-bar chairs. Two published bar ranges look contradictory but are not: light slab chairs are commonly fabricated from 8–10 mm bars, while heavy cage chair bars are usually 12 mm and above (12/16/20 mm), because a thin chair bar buckles under a loaded cage. Match the product to the load, and confirm it against the drawing.

How many spare chairs should I order?

Add about 5% and always round UP — you cannot buy half a chair, and rounding down loses one. The defaults order becomes 30 × 1.05 = 31.5 → 32 chairs; the 10 × 8 m example becomes 99 × 1.05 = 103.95 → 104 chairs. Spares cover chairs crushed or knocked over during fixing and the pour.

Does the calculator include cost or weight?

No — it gives the neat grid count only. Price it as count × your unit price: 30 chairs at an example €2.00 each is €60.00 before spares, and 99 chairs is €198.00. As a weight sense-check only, published guide figures put one steel chair at roughly 0.15–0.25 kg, so 30 steel chairs weigh about 4.5–7.5 kg. For a full takeoff use the quantity & takeoff calculator, and price it with the cost calculator.
