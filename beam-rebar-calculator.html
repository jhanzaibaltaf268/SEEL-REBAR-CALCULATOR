How to use: Beam Rebar Calculator

  1. Enter beam length, width, depth and clear cover.
  2. Set the number of top bars and bottom bars, and pick the main bar size.
  3. Set stirrup spacing and stirrup bar size.
  4. Read main steel, stirrup steel, the stirrup count and the total weight.
  5. Add laps, hooks and anchorage separately — the calculator counts straight bars at full beam length only, then allow waste before ordering.

Formula

Main steel = (top + bottom bars) × length × kg/m. Stirrups = floor(length ÷ spacing) + 1 at the stirrup cutting length.

The formula, explained

A beam's steel is two piles added together: straight main bars, and the closed stirrups wrapped around them. The calculator keeps them separate so you can check each one against your bar bending schedule.

Main steel = (top bars + bottom bars) × beam length × kg/m. On the defaults: (2 + 3) × 5 m × 1.578 kg/m = 39.45 kg. Split by layer, the top bars are 2 × 5 × 1.578 = 15.78 kg and the bottom bars are 3 × 5 × 1.578 = 23.67 kg. The kg/m values come from d² ÷ 162.28: 16 mm gives 256 ÷ 162.28 = 1.578 kg/m.

Stirrup steel = number of stirrups × cutting length × kg/m. The count is floor(beam length in mm ÷ spacing) + 1 — the +1 is the stirrup at the far end that a plain length ÷ spacing division misses. On the defaults: floor(5000 ÷ 150) + 1 = 34 stirrups. The cutting length works from the inside of the stirrup: a = 230 − 2 × 25 = 180 mm, b = 450 − 2 × 25 = 400 mm, then cut = 2(180 + 400) + 20 × 8 − 6 × 8 − 6 × 8 = 1224 mm. That is two 10d hooks added, three 2d 90° bends and two 3d 135° bends deducted — the same formula as the stirrup calculator. Each stirrup weighs 1.224 × 0.394 = 0.48 kg, so stirrup steel is 34 × 1.224 × 0.394 = 16.40 kg, and the total is 39.45 + 16.40 = 55.85 kg (0.056 ton). The panel computes 8 mm stirrups at 0.394 kg/m, the option value for 8 mm.

Two things the formula does not do. It does not add anything to the main-bar length: no lap, no hook, no development length, no bearing length. And it does not check whether the bars you entered are enough — bar counts and sizes come from the structural drawing, and this tool only weighs them.

Worked examples

Example 1 — the calculator defaults. A 5 m beam, 230 × 450 mm, 25 mm cover, 2 top + 3 bottom bars of 16 mm, 8 mm stirrups at 150 mm. Main steel 39.45 kg, stirrup steel 16.40 kg, 34 stirrups at 1224 mm each, total 55.85 kg (0.056 ton). Total bar lengths: mains 25.00 m, stirrups 41.62 m. Try it yourself: enter the defaults and the panel shows exactly these rows.

Example 2 — a larger beam. A 6 m beam, 300 × 600 mm, 30 mm cover, 2 top + 4 bottom bars of 20 mm, 10 mm stirrups at 200 mm. Main steel is 6 × 6 × 2.465 = 88.74 kg. Stirrups: floor(6000 ÷ 200) + 1 = 31; a = 300 − 60 = 240 mm, b = 600 − 60 = 540 mm, cut = 2(240 + 540) + 200 − 60 − 60 = 1640 mm; each stirrup weighs 1.640 × 0.617 = 1.01 kg, so stirrup steel is 31 × 1.640 × 0.617 = 31.37 kg. Total 120.11 kg (0.120 ton). Total bar lengths: mains 36.00 m, stirrups 50.84 m.

Example 3 — a small lintel-style beam. A 4 m beam, 230 × 300 mm, 25 mm cover, 2 top + 2 bottom bars of 12 mm, 8 mm stirrups at 125 mm. Main steel is 4 × 4 × 0.887 = 14.19 kg. Stirrups: floor(4000 ÷ 125) + 1 = 33; a = 180 mm, b = 250 mm, cut = 2(180 + 250) + 160 − 48 − 48 = 924 mm; each stirrup weighs 0.924 × 0.394 = 0.36 kg, so stirrup steel is 33 × 0.924 × 0.394 = 12.01 kg. Total 26.21 kg (0.026 ton). Total bar lengths: mains 16.00 m, stirrups 30.49 m.

Main bar size

Main steel grows with the square of the diameter, so one size step is a big jump. Holding everything else at the defaults (5 m, 2 top + 3 bottom bars, 8 mm stirrups at 150 mm, stirrup steel 16.40 kg throughout):

Main barkg/mMain steelTotal
12 mm0.88722.18 kg38.57 kg
16 mm1.57839.45 kg55.85 kg
20 mm2.46561.63 kg78.02 kg
25 mm3.85196.28 kg112.67 kg

Going from 16 mm to 20 mm mains adds 22.18 kg to a single 5 m beam — over 55% more main steel. That is why substituting a larger bar "to be safe" is expensive, and why a smaller bar is never a safe substitution: the area matters, not just the weight. If your drawing specifies different sizes top and bottom (say 20 mm bottom, 12 mm top), the calculator cannot take two main sizes in one run — run it once with the top bars set to zero and once with the bottom bars set to zero, using the matching size each time, and add the two main-steel figures. The stirrup steel is identical in both runs, so add it only once.

Stirrup spacing

Stirrup spacing moves the total less than main-bar size does, but it is the number most often guessed wrong. Holding everything else at the defaults (main steel 39.45 kg throughout):

SpacingStirrupsStirrup steelTotal

150 mm3416.40 kg55.85 kg
200 mm2612.54 kg51.99 kg

300 mm178.20 kg47.65 kg

Halving the spacing from 150 mm to 75 mm almost doubles stirrup steel (16.40 kg to 32.31 kg); opening it to 300 mm saves only 8.20 kg against the defaults. And 300 mm is not a free saving: IS 456:2000 caps shear-stirrup spacing at the lesser of 0.75d or 300 mm. On the defaults, d ≈ 409 mm (450 mm depth minus 25 mm cover, 8 mm stirrup and half a 16 mm bar), so 0.75d = 306.75 mm and the 300 mm cap governs — the 300 mm row sits exactly at the legal limit for this section, and on a shallower beam it would already exceed it. Take spacing from the drawing; use this table to price the difference, not to choose the spacing.

Cover and stirrup cutting length

Cover shrinks the stirrup, so more cover means slightly less stirrup steel — but cover is durability, not a saving to chase. Holding everything else at the defaults (main steel 39.45 kg, 34 stirrups of 8 mm throughout):

CoverInner a × bCut lengthStirrup steelTotal
20 mm190 × 410 mm1264 mm16.93 kg56.38 kg
25 mm180 × 400 mm1224 mm16.40 kg55.85 kg
30 mm170 × 390 mm1184 mm15.86 kg55.31 kg
40 mm150 × 370 mm1104 mm14.79 kg54.24 kg
50 mm130 × 350 mm1024 mm13.72 kg53.17 kg

Each extra 5 mm of cover shrinks a + b by 20 mm (10 mm on each dimension), so the cut drops 40 mm from 25 mm to 30 mm cover and 80 mm from 30 mm to 40 mm. The whole range from 20 mm to 50 mm cover moves the beam total by only 3.21 kg — never worth trading against the cover your exposure condition requires. The common error runs the other way: working the cut from the OUTER dimensions (230 × 450 mm) gives 2(230 + 450) + 160 − 48 − 48 = 1424 mm instead of 1224 mm, which overstates every stirrup by 200 mm — 6.80 m over 34 stirrups, 2.68 kg of steel that does not exist.

Support zones vs midspan

Shear is highest near the supports, so drawings commonly call for closer stirrups over the end zones and wider spacing at midspan — for example 100 mm near the supports and 200 mm in the middle. The calculator takes one spacing, so run it once per zone and add the stirrup weights. On the defaults section, with 1 m end zones at 100 mm and a 3 m middle zone at 200 mm: each end zone gives floor(1000 ÷ 100) + 1 = 11 stirrups and 11 × 1.224 × 0.394 = 5.30 kg; the middle zone gives floor(3000 ÷ 200) + 1 = 16 stirrups and 16 × 1.224 × 0.394 = 7.72 kg. That sums to 38 counts and 18.33 kg, but the stirrups at the two zone boundaries are counted twice — subtract them (2 × 1.224 × 0.394 = 0.96 kg) for 36 stirrups and 17.36 kg of stirrup steel. Against the uniform 150 mm spacing (34 stirrups, 16.40 kg), the zoned layout costs 0.96 kg more on this beam — a small price for the shear capacity at the supports. Main steel is unaffected: add the zones' stirrup steel to the single main-steel figure, not to each zone's panel total.

Steel percentage check

The calculator weighs whatever bars you enter; it never asks whether they are enough. The quick check is the steel percentage: total bar area ÷ gross concrete area. One 16 mm bar is π/4 × 16² = 201.06 mm², so the defaults' 5 bars give 1005.31 mm² over 230 × 450 = 103500 mm², which is 0.97%. The bottom bars alone (3 × 201.06 = 603.19 mm²) are 0.58% of gross. Example 2 gives 1884.96 mm² over 180000 mm² = 1.05%; example 3 gives 452.39 mm² over 69000 mm² = 0.66%.

IS 456:2000 brackets the answer: tension steel must be at least 0.85/fy of b×d (for Fe415, 0.205% of b×d), and total steel must not exceed 4% of the gross area. On the defaults, b×d = 230 × 409 = 94070 mm², so the minimum is 192.67 mm² and the maximum is 4140.00 mm²; on example 2 the maximum is 7200.00 mm². All three worked examples sit inside their brackets — but passing this bracket is not a design. It only catches gross errors, like a beam entered with one bar or with an absurd bar count. Work out the required area from the bending moment (As ≈ M ÷ (0.87 × fy × d)) or take it from the drawing, and check bar combinations on the area calculator.

A second sanity check is weight per cubic metre of concrete. The defaults beam holds 0.5175 m³ of concrete (5 × 0.23 × 0.45), so 55.85 kg is 107.92 kg/m³. Example 2 is 111.21 kg/m³ and example 3 is 94.95 kg/m³. Published early-estimate bands for beams run about 170–250 lb/yd³, which converts to 100.86–148.32 kg/m³ — the defaults and example 2 sit inside that band, and example 3 sits just below it, which is reasonable for a lightly loaded small beam. A figure far outside the band usually means a wrong input, not a special beam.

Laps and anchorage

Two additions turn the panel total into an order quantity, and both are missing from the calculator by design. First, laps: any bar run longer than stock length (usually 12 m) needs a splice. Take a 13 m beam with everything else at the defaults — the panel gives 144.53 kg (main 102.57 kg, stirrup 41.96 kg, 87 stirrups). One 16 mm Fe500 M25 tension lap of 0.777 m per bar adds 5 × 0.777 × 1.578 = 6.13 kg, taking main steel to 108.70 kg. Get the lap from the lap length calculator; do not use a memorised multiple, since the lap depends on grade, concrete and tension vs compression.

Second, anchorage: main bars must develop into their supports, and the calculator counts them straight at beam length with nothing added. For scale, the development length of a 16 mm Fe500 bar in M25 concrete is 582.59 mm (see the development length calculator), and a standard 90° end hook adds 9d (see the hook cutting length calculator). Cranked (bent-up) bars, extra top bars over supports, and curtailed bottom bars are the same story: they change the bar lengths, so work them out on the detailing pages and add the weight. A beam schedule that ignores laps and anchorage under-orders by roughly the lap allowance plus the hook allowance on every bar — on a multi-beam floor, that is kilograms per beam turning into a missed delivery.

Ordering and cost

Order per beam type, then multiply. Ten beams at the defaults total 10 × 55.85 = 558.47 kg; with a 5% waste allowance for offcuts that is 586.39 kg, which costs €703.67 at an example €1.20/kg (prices are examples — enter your local rate on the cost calculator). For stock lengths, convert bar lengths, not weights: the defaults need 25.00 m of 16 mm main bars, which is 3 stock bars of 12 m (2 bars cut per stock bar, 5 needed), and 41.62 m of 8 mm stirrup bars, which is 4 stock bars of 12 m. Example 2 needs exactly 3 stock bars for its 36.00 m of mains and 5 for its 50.84 m of stirrups. In tonnage, the defaults beam is 0.056 ton, so 1000 ÷ 55.85 = 17.91 beams make a tonne — convert any schedule on the tonnage calculator, and build a full count-and-length takeoff on the quantity calculator. Tie wire for fixing the cage is separate: estimate it on the tie wire calculator.

Imperial units

The bar dropdowns include US sizes, so the same calculator works in imperial — just enter the metric equivalents of your dimensions. A 5 m beam (16.40 ft) of 230 × 450 mm (9.06 × 17.72 in) with 2 top + 3 bottom #5 bars (15.9 mm, 1.552 kg/m) and #3 stirrups (9.5 mm, 0.56 kg/m) at 152.4 mm (6 in) spacing gives: main steel 5 × 5 × 1.552 = 38.80 kg (85.54 lb); stirrups floor(5000 ÷ 152.4) + 1 = 33, cut 2(180 + 400) + 8 × 9.5 = 1236 mm (48.66 in), stirrup steel 33 × 1.236 × 0.56 = 22.84 kg (50.35 lb); total 61.64 kg (135.89 lb). Note the stirrup count drops by one against the 150 mm metric spacing (33 vs 34) because 6 in is slightly wider than 150 mm — small spacing differences move the count, which is why the spacing table above matters. ACI 318 cover for beams not exposed to weather is 1.5 in to the ties, against the 25 mm used here.

What this calculator does not include

This is a weighing tool, not a design tool, and the following are all outside it: the structural design itself (bending moment, shear, required steel area, deflection, beam depth and width — all from the engineer); lap lengths; hooks, cranks and development/anchorage lengths; extra top bars over supports and curtailment of bottom bars; side-face reinforcement for deep beams over 750 mm; T-beam or L-beam flange steel; concrete volume, formwork and labour; cover blocks, chairs and spacers; tie wire; and waste. Use the companion pages for the missing pieces: development length, lap length, hooks, stirrup cutting length, columns (the beam frames into them — check the joint), area, quantity, cost and tonnage.

Common mistakes

1. Entering the clear span instead of the beam length. The calculator counts main bars at the entered length, so a 4.6 m clear span entered for a 5 m beam (with bearings) understates main steel by 0.4 m per bar — 5 × 0.4 × 1.578 = 3.16 kg on the defaults — before laps and anchorage are even considered. Enter the length the bars actually run, then add laps and hooks.

2. Working the stirrup cut from the outside of the beam. The cut runs around the inside of the stirrup (width and depth minus twice the cover), plus hooks minus bend deductions. Using outer dimensions gives 1424 mm instead of 1224 mm on the defaults — 2.68 kg of phantom stirrup steel per beam.

3. Using one spacing for a zoned drawing. A drawing that calls for 100 mm at the supports and 200 mm at midspan cannot be entered as a single 150 mm spacing without error: on the defaults section the zoned layout needs 36 stirrups (17.36 kg) against 34 (16.40 kg) uniform. Run each zone separately and correct for the shared boundary stirrups, as shown above.

4. Forgetting laps on long beams. A 13 m beam needs a splice in every main bar; skipping the 0.777 m lap per bar understates main steel by 6.13 kg on the defaults section — and by that amount on every long beam in the schedule.

5. Ordering the panel total with no waste allowance. The panel total is net steel: no offcuts, no cutting waste, no short bars, no laps or hooks. Add 3–5% for straight work (more with laps and hooks) and round up to 12 m stock bars before placing the order, or the last beam on the floor comes up short.

Beam Rebar Calculator — FAQ

How do you calculate beam reinforcement?

Add the main bar weight to the stirrup weight along the beam length. Main steel = (top bars + bottom bars) × beam length × kg/m. Stirrup steel = number of stirrups × stirrup cutting length × kg/m. On the calculator defaults (5 m beam, 2 top + 3 bottom bars of 16 mm, 8 mm stirrups at 150 mm), that is 39.45 kg of main steel + 16.40 kg of stirrup steel = 55.85 kg.

What is the spacing of stirrups in a beam?

Often closer near supports and wider at midspan; commonly 100–200 mm — design governs. As a code ceiling, IS 456:2000 limits shear-stirrup spacing to the lesser of 0.75d or 300 mm, where d is the effective depth. On the calculator defaults (d ≈ 409 mm), 0.75d = 306.75 mm, so 300 mm governs — the 300 mm row in the spacing table below sits exactly at that limit. Never pick a spacing above the maximum on your drawing.

Why does a beam need both top and bottom bars?

Because bending puts one face in tension and the other in compression, and the tension side flips along the beam. At midspan of a simply supported beam the bottom is in tension, so the bottom bars do the main work (23.67 kg of the defaults' 39.45 kg main steel). Over a support in a continuous beam the top is in tension, so top bars carry that hogging moment. In a simply supported beam the 2 top bars are often just hanger bars that hold the stirrups in place — but they are still counted, still weighed, and still ordered.

How do you calculate the stirrup cutting length?

From the inside of the stirrup, not the outside of the beam: a = width − 2 × cover, b = depth − 2 × cover, then cut = 2(a + b) + 20d − 6d − 6d (two 10d hooks, minus three 2d 90° bends and two 3d 135° bends). On the defaults (230 × 450 mm, 25 mm cover, 8 mm stirrups): a = 180 mm, b = 400 mm, cut = 2(180 + 400) + 160 − 48 − 48 = 1224 mm. The same formula drives the stirrup cutting length calculator.

What is the minimum reinforcement for a beam?

IS 456:2000 requires tension steel of at least 0.85/fy of b×d — for Fe415 that is 0.205% of b×d — and caps total steel at 4% of the gross section. On the defaults (b×d = 230 × 409 = 94070 mm²), the minimum is 192.67 mm² and the maximum is 4140.00 mm²; the defaults' 1005.31 mm² (0.97% of gross) sits between them. This calculator does not check any of this — check your steel area on the area calculator and take bar counts from the structural drawing, never from this tool alone.

Do I need to add lap length to a beam?

Yes, whenever a bar run is longer than the stock length (usually 12 m) or a bar is spliced. This calculator counts every main bar at the entered beam length with no lap added. A 13 m beam on the defaults otherwise gives 102.57 kg of main steel; one 16 mm Fe500 M25 tension lap of 0.777 m per bar adds 5 × 0.777 × 1.578 = 6.13 kg, taking main steel to 108.70 kg. Get the lap from the lap length calculator and add it before ordering.

Does the calculator include hooks and development length?

No. Main bars are counted straight at full beam length — no end hooks, no anchorage into the support, no cranked (bent-up) bars. For scale, the development length of a 16 mm Fe500 bar in M25 concrete is 582.59 mm (see the development length calculator), and a standard 90° hook adds 9d (see the hook cutting length calculator). If your bars are hooked or cranked, work out those lengths there and add the weight.

How do I handle different stirrup spacing near supports?

The calculator takes one spacing for the whole beam, so run it once per zone and add the stirrup weights. Example on the defaults section: 1 m at each end at 100 mm gives 11 stirrups per end, and the middle 3 m at 200 mm gives 16 — 38 counts, minus the 2 stirrups shared at the zone boundaries, is 36 stirrups and 17.36 kg of stirrup steel, against 16.40 kg for a uniform 150 mm spacing. The boundary correction matters: counting all 38 would overstate stirrup steel by 0.96 kg.

What is a doubly reinforced beam, and does this calculator cover it?

A doubly reinforced beam has compression steel at the top in addition to tension steel at the bottom, used when the section is too shallow for singly reinforced design. For weighing purposes this calculator covers it fine — top and bottom bars are counted together — but it does not design anything: it cannot tell you whether compression steel is needed, what a T-beam flange contributes, or what side-face bars a deep beam (over 750 mm per IS 456) needs. Those come from the structural design.

How much steel should I actually order for a beam?

Take the panel total, add laps, hooks and any extra support bars, then add a waste allowance (3–5% covers offcuts on straight work; use the higher end when laps and hooks are involved) and round up to stock lengths. Ten beams at the defaults total 558.47 kg; with 5% waste that is 586.39 kg, which is €703.67 at an example €1.20/kg. Price it on the cost calculator and convert it on the tonnage calculator (the defaults beam is 0.056 ton, so 17.91 beams make a tonne).
