How to use: Wall Rebar Calculator

  1. Enter the wall length and height in metres, and the edge cover in mm.
  2. Set the vertical spacing and bar size, and the horizontal spacing and bar size.
  3. Read the vertical and horizontal bar counts and weights, plus the total.
  4. Double the total for walls thicker than 200 mm, which need two grids — the calculator counts one.
  5. Add laps, dowels and waste before ordering — the calculator counts straight, cover-reduced bars only.

Formula

Vertical bars = floor((L − 2cover) ÷ spV) + 1; horizontal bars = floor((H − 2cover) ÷ spH) + 1; sum × kg/m.

The formula, explained

A wall grid is two one-dimensional counts laid across each other — verticals counted along the length, horizontals counted up the height — and the calculator keeps them separate so you can see which direction dominates:

Vertical bars = floor((L − 2cover) ÷ spV) + 1, each (H − 2cover) long; Horizontal bars = floor((H − 2cover) ÷ spH) + 1, each (L − 2cover) long; Total = vertical steel + horizontal steel

Both counts use the same fence-post rule as every grid on this site: the cover-reduced run divided by the spacing gives the number of intervals, and the bars number one more than the intervals, because both end bars count. And both bar lengths are cover-reduced — the verticals stop 40 mm short of the top and bottom on the defaults, the horizontals 40 mm short of each end. That is a real difference from the beam and column pages, which count bars at full member length: a wall bar is cut to fit inside the wall, so the calculator cuts it. One display note, carried over from those pages: the vertical-bar field reads "0.888|12" and the horizontal-bar field "0.617|10", but the panel computes with the option values 0.887 and 0.616 kg/m, so every weight below uses 0.887 and 0.616.

Worked example 1: defaults (6 × 3 m)

Open the calculator and leave the defaults: length 6 m, height 3 m, vertical spacing 200 mm, horizontal spacing 200 mm, edge cover 40 mm, vertical bars 12 mm, horizontal bars 10 mm.

Verticals = floor((6 − 0.08) ÷ 0.2) + 1 = floor(29.6) + 1 = 30 bars, each 3 − 0.08 = 2.92 m long → 30 × 2.92 × 0.887 = 77.70 kg. Horizontals = floor((3 − 0.08) ÷ 0.2) + 1 = floor(14.6) + 1 = 15 bars, each 6 − 0.08 = 5.92 m long → 15 × 5.92 × 0.616 = 54.70 kg.

Total = 77.70 + 54.70 = 132.40 kg (0.132 t). The panel shows 132.40 kg, 30.00 vertical bars, 15.00 horizontal bars, 77.70 kg vertical steel, 54.70 kg horizontal steel and 0.132 t. Vertical steel is 58.7% of the total here — on most walls the verticals dominate, because there are usually more of them and they are the heavier bar. Total bar length is 30 × 2.92 + 15 × 5.92 = 176.40 m, and at 18 m² of wall face that is 7.36 kg/m² — a useful density to compare against the next examples.

Worked example 2: 10 × 4 m wall

Set length 10 m, height 4 m, vertical spacing 150 mm, horizontal spacing 150 mm, cover 40 mm, vertical bars 16 mm, horizontal bars 12 mm.

Verticals = floor((10 − 0.08) ÷ 0.15) + 1 = 67 bars, each 4 − 0.08 = 3.92 m → 67 × 3.92 × 1.578 = 414.45 kg. Horizontals = floor((4 − 0.08) ÷ 0.15) + 1 = 27 bars, each 10 − 0.08 = 9.92 m → 27 × 9.92 × 0.887 = 237.57 kg.

Total = 414.45 + 237.57 = 652.02 kg (0.652 t). At 40 m² of wall face that is 16.30 kg/m² — more than double the defaults density, because both spacings tightened and both bar sizes stepped up at once. Note the horizontal bars here run 9.92 m, still inside a 12 m stock length — no splice needed. Stretch this wall to 20 m and that stops being true (see the order section).

Worked example 3: small garden wall

Set length 4 m, height 2.4 m, vertical spacing 400 mm, horizontal spacing 400 mm, cover 40 mm, vertical bars 12 mm, horizontal bars 10 mm.

Verticals = floor((4 − 0.08) ÷ 0.4) + 1 = 10 bars, each 2.4 − 0.08 = 2.32 m → 10 × 2.32 × 0.887 = 20.58 kg. Horizontals = floor((2.4 − 0.08) ÷ 0.4) + 1 = 6 bars, each 4 − 0.08 = 3.92 m → 6 × 3.92 × 0.616 = 14.49 kg.

Total = 20.58 + 14.49 = 35.07 kg (0.035 t). At 9.6 m² that is only 3.65 kg/m² — a lightly reinforced garden wall, and the 400 mm spacings are fine against the code cap only if the wall is thin enough: the cap is the smaller of 3 × thickness and 450 mm, so a 150 mm wall caps spacing at 450 mm and 400 mm passes, but a 100 mm wall caps it at 300 mm and 400 mm would fail. Spacing limits always need the thickness, which this calculator does not ask for — another reason the check belongs in the text, not just the tool.

Vertical spacing

Same defaults wall (6 × 3 m, 12 mm verticals, 10 mm horizontals @ 200 mm giving a constant 54.70 kg), five vertical spacings. The horizontal steel never moves, so the whole table is the vertical half doing its thing.

Two readings. First, spacing is a strong lever: tightening from 200 mm to 100 mm doubles the vertical count and adds 77.70 kg (+59%) to one wall. Second, there are dead bands: any vertical spacing from 198 mm to 204 mm gives the same 30 bars on this wall — 204 mm returns exactly the defaults total of 132.40 kg — while 205 mm drops to 29 bars and 129.81 kg. So a spacing "improvement" of a few millimetres often changes nothing, and a spacing quoted to the millimetre without the count is meaningless. Always read the count, not just the spacing.

Horizontal steel is the smaller half on this wall, so even doubling its spacing (200 mm → 400 mm) saves only 25.53 kg. But spacing is not a free saving either: the code cap for a 200 mm wall is 450 mm, so the 400 mm row passes, while on a 100 mm wall the cap is 300 mm and both the 400 mm and (just) the 300 mm rows would need checking against the drawing. And remember the minimum-steel check below: widening the horizontal spacing is exactly how a grid quietly falls below the 0.20% horizontal minimum.

The minimum-steel check

The calculator weighs whatever grid you enter; it never asks whether the grid is enough. The check needs the wall thickness, which the calculator does not ask for — so here it is, worked by hand. IS 456:2000 cl. 32.5 requires vertical steel ≥ 0.12% and horizontal steel ≥ 0.20% of the gross concrete area (for deformed bars ≤ 16 mm, Fe415 or better), with both spacings no wider than the smaller of 3 × wall thickness and 450 mm. Steel per metre of wall is bar area ÷ spacing: a 12 mm bar is 113.10 mm², a 10 mm bar 78.54 mm².

On a 200 mm thick wall the defaults grid gives vertical steel of 113.10 ÷ 0.2 = 565.5 mm²/m, which is 565.5 ÷ 200000 = 0.283% — comfortably above the 0.12% minimum. But the horizontals give 78.54 ÷ 0.2 = 392.7 mm²/m, which is 392.7 ÷ 200000 = 0.196% — just BELOW the 0.20% horizontal minimum. The defaults grid, as it stands, fails the horizontal check on a 200 mm wall by four thousandths of a percent. The fix is small: tighten the horizontals to 190 mm (78.54 ÷ 0.19 = 413.4 mm²/m = 0.207%, panel total 136.05 kg) or step the horizontal bar up to 12 mm at 200 mm (565.5 mm²/m = 0.283%). Either fix costs a few kilograms; the uncorrected grid costs a failed check.

Two published examples fail worse. One guide's shear-wall example (12 mm @ 300 mm vertical, 10 mm @ 450 mm horizontal, 200 mm wall) gives verticals of 377.0 mm²/m = 0.189% (passes) but horizontals of only 174.5 mm²/m = 0.087% (fails by more than half), and its stated vertical maximum spacing of 600 mm exceeds the code cap of 450 mm for that wall. And a design example worked elsewhere on this site's research — a retaining wall needing 480 mm²/m of vertical steel — is satisfied by 12 mm bars at any spacing up to 113.10 ÷ 480 = 0.236 m, so 12 mm @ 200 mm (565.5 mm²/m) passes with margin. That is the bridge between a design number and a grid: required area per metre ÷ bar area = maximum spacing.

For reference, the ACI 318-19 minima quoted by one published wall tool (0.0015 vertical, 0.0012 horizontal) are looser on the horizontal side than IS 456 — the defaults grid passes both ACI minima. The draft uses the stricter IS figures as the check, because this site's audience builds to IS 456; if your project is ACI-governed, confirm which minima your drawing cites.

One grid or two?

This is the scope limit most likely to halve your order. The calculator counts one grid: one layer of verticals, one layer of horizontals. IS 456 requires two grids — one near each face — for walls thicker than 200 mm. So a 250 mm retaining wall or basement wall at the defaults grid needs 2 × 132.40 = 264.80 kg, not 132.40 kg. The calculator has no thickness input and no two-curtain switch, so the doubling is yours to remember.

Worked retaining example, single grid first: an 8 m long, 3.5 m high wall, verticals 16 mm @ 150 mm, horizontals 12 mm @ 200 mm, cover 50 mm. Verticals = floor((8 − 0.10) ÷ 0.15) + 1 = 53 bars, each 3.5 − 0.10 = 3.40 m → 53 × 3.40 × 1.578 = 284.36 kg. Horizontals = floor((3.5 − 0.10) ÷ 0.2) + 1 = 18 bars, each 8 − 0.10 = 7.90 m → 18 × 7.90 × 0.887 = 126.13 kg. Total, one grid = 410.49 kg (0.410 t), or 14.66 kg/m² over 28 m². If that wall's stem is thicker than 200 mm — as a 3.5 m retaining stem usually is — double it: 820.97 kg for two grids, before laps, dowels and waste. And a retaining wall is more than its stem: the base slab (toe and heel), any counterforts and the starter dowels into the footing are separate schedules — take the footing steel from the footing calculator and the stem-to-footing dowels from the drawing, never from this grid.

From one wall to the order

The calculator gives one wall, one grid, neat. The order is that wall, times the number of identical walls, times the number of grids, plus laps and waste.

Long walls need splices first. A 20 m wall at the defaults grid gives 100 verticals and 15 horizontals, 259.00 + 184.06 = 443.06 kg — but the horizontal bars run 19.92 m, well past a 12 m stock length, so each needs one splice. At roughly 0.486 m for a 10 mm Fe500 M25 tension lap (get the exact figure from the lap length calculator), that adds 15 × 0.486 × 0.616 = 4.49 kg. Vertical bars at 2.92 m need no splice.

Then stock lengths. The defaults need 30 vertical bars of 2.92 m: a 12 m stock bar yields 4, so 30 bars need 8 stock bars of 12 mm. The horizontals need 15 bars of 5.92 m: a 12 m stock bar yields 2, so 15 bars need 8 stock bars of 10 mm. Sixteen stock bars in total for one defaults wall — and offcuts of 0.32 m per vertical stock bar and 0.16 m per horizontal stock bar, which is where the waste allowance goes.

Then the money. Ten walls at the defaults total 10 × 132.40 = 1324.02 kg; with a 5% waste allowance that is 1390.22 kg, which costs €1668.27 at an example €1.20/kg (prices are examples — enter your local rate on the cost calculator). Convert any schedule on the tonnage calculator, and build a full count-and-length takeoff on the quantity calculator. Tie wire for fixing the grid is separate: estimate it on the tie wire calculator.

Imperial conversions

The calculator takes metres and millimetres, so convert first: 1 m = 3.28 ft, 1 kg = 2.20 lb, 1 in = 25.4 mm.

Worked conversions: the defaults total 132.40 kg = 291.90 lb (0.132 t); a 6 m wall is 19.69 ft long, a 3 m wall 9.84 ft high, 200 mm spacing is 7.87 in and 40 mm cover is 1.57 in. A full imperial run — a 20 × 10 ft wall (6.096 × 3.048 m), 6 in spacing both ways, 1.5 in cover, #4 verticals (12.7 mm, 0.994 kg/m) and #3 horizontals (9.5 mm, 0.56 kg/m) — gives 40 verticals and 20 horizontals, 118.16 + 67.42 = 185.58 kg (409.13 lb). Note the vertical count: 6.096 m at 6 in spacing gives 40 bars, where the same wall at 150 mm spacing would give 41 — small spacing differences move the count, which is why the spacing tables above matter. The bar dropdowns include US sizes, so enter the metric equivalents of your dimensions and pick the matching US bar.

What this calculator does not cover

  • Structural design — earth pressure, sliding and overturning stability, shear, bending moment and the required steel area; the minimum-steel check above catches gross errors, it does not prove the wall stands up.
  • Second grids — walls thicker than 200 mm need two curtains; the calculator counts one (see the two-grid section).
  • Base slab, toe, heel and counterforts on retaining walls — separate schedules, not this grid.
  • Dowels and starter bars into the footing or slab — see the footing calculator and the drawing.
  • Laps, hooks and bends at corners, ends and openings — see the lap length calculator and the hook cutting length calculator.
  • Openings — doors and windows are not deducted; subtract the bars they displace by hand and add the trimming bars from the drawing.
  • Block-wall coursing — the grid model approximates cores and bond beams; check the spacing against the block module (see the block-wall FAQ).
  • Concrete volume, formwork, chairs and spacers — the concrete estimator covers volume; chairs are on the chair calculator.
  • Cost, tonnage and multi-wall takeoff — the order section shows the hand arithmetic; the cost calculator prices a full takeoff, the tonnage calculator converts it, and the quantity & takeoff calculator totals across members.

5 common mistakes

  1. Forgetting the +1 on both counts. Length ÷ spacing without the +1 gives 29 verticals instead of 30 and 14 horizontals instead of 15 on the defaults — 2.59 + 3.65 = 6.24 kg missing from every wall. Both end bars are real bars.
  2. Counting full lengths instead of cover-reduced lengths. Verticals run height minus twice the cover (2.92 m, not 3.00 m) and horizontals length minus twice the cover (5.92 m, not 6.00 m). Using full lengths overstates the defaults wall by 2.13 + 0.74 = 2.87 kg — steel that does not exist and bars that do not fit with their cover intact.
  3. Ordering one grid for a two-grid wall. Any wall thicker than 200 mm needs two curtains under IS 456. Ordering the calculator's 132.40 kg for a 250 mm wall delivers half the steel. Double it: 264.80 kg.
  4. Trusting a spacing without the count. Spacings from 198 mm to 204 mm all give the same 30 verticals on the defaults wall, so a "200 mm" grid and a "204 mm" grid weigh the same — but 205 mm drops a bar. Always read the panel's bar count after changing a spacing.
  5. Forgetting splices on long walls. Horizontal bars longer than the 12 m stock length need a lap each. On a 20 m wall that is 4.49 kg of lap steel the neat total omits — and on every long wall in the schedule, the same omission repeats.

Wall Rebar Calculator — FAQ

How do you calculate rebar for a wall?

Count vertical and horizontal bars from the spacing, then multiply lengths by unit weight. Vertical bars = floor((length − 2 × cover) ÷ vertical spacing) + 1, each (height − 2 × cover) long; horizontal bars = floor((height − 2 × cover) ÷ horizontal spacing) + 1, each (length − 2 × cover) long. On the calculator defaults (6 × 3 m wall, 12 mm verticals @ 200 mm, 10 mm horizontals @ 200 mm, 40 mm cover) that is 30 verticals at 2.92 m = 77.70 kg plus 15 horizontals at 5.92 m = 54.70 kg, total 132.40 kg.

What is rebar spacing in a block wall?

Often vertical bars at 400–800 mm and horizontal bars per course or bond beam — design governs. For a concrete wall the usual residential grid is much tighter: 200 mm each way on the calculator defaults, and published guides quote 12 in (about 300 mm) vertical and horizontal as a common grid, or 36 in vertical / 24 in horizontal for lightly loaded walls. The spacing on your drawing always wins over any of these rules of thumb.

How many vertical bars does a wall need?

One more than the number of spacings that fit: floor((length − 2 × cover) ÷ spacing) + 1. The +1 is the bar at the far edge, and forgetting it is the classic off-by-one — on the defaults it drops the verticals from 30 to 29 and understates the wall by 2.59 kg, and the horizontals from 15 to 14 for another 3.65 kg, 6.24 kg short in total. The calculator counts both end bars.

What is the minimum reinforcement in a wall?

IS 456:2000 cl. 32.5 sets vertical steel at ≥ 0.12% and horizontal steel at ≥ 0.20% of the gross concrete area (for deformed bars ≤ 16 mm, Fe415 or better), with both spacings ≤ the smaller of 3 × wall thickness and 450 mm. On a 200 mm wall that cap is 450 mm. The defaults pass on the vertical side (12 mm @ 200 mm = 0.283%) but the horizontals (10 mm @ 200 mm = 0.196%) sit just below the 0.20% minimum — tighten them to 190 mm (0.207%) or step up to 12 mm. This calculator does not check any of this; work the percentages by hand for your wall thickness.

Do I need two layers of steel in a wall?

For walls thicker than 200 mm, yes — IS 456 requires the vertical and horizontal reinforcement in two grids, one near each face. This calculator counts ONE grid, so a 250 mm wall at the defaults grid needs 2 × 132.40 = 264.80 kg, not 132.40 kg. For walls 200 mm or thinner a single central grid is the usual arrangement, but the drawing governs.

Do I need to add lap length to a wall?

Yes, whenever a bar run exceeds the stock length (usually 12 m). The calculator counts every bar at its cover-reduced length with no splice added. On a 20 m wall the horizontal bars run 19.92 m, so each needs one splice: at roughly 0.486 m for a 10 mm Fe500 M25 tension lap, that adds 15 × 0.486 × 0.616 = 4.49 kg. Get the exact lap from the lap length calculator — it depends on bar size, grade, concrete and tension vs compression, never on a memorised multiple.

Does the calculator design the wall?

No — it weighs a grid you describe; it does not check whether the grid is adequate. Earth pressure, sliding and overturning stability, shear, bending and the required steel area all come from the structural design and the drawing. A total that looks reasonable is not proof the wall stands up, and passing the minimum-steel percentages is not proof it carries the soil behind it.

What about doors, windows and other openings?

The calculator counts the full rectangle — it does not deduct openings. For each opening, work out the bars that would have crossed it and subtract them, then add the trimming bars the drawing calls for around the opening. One published wall tool deducts openings from volume automatically; here you do the deduction by hand before ordering.

Can I use this calculator for a block wall?

As an estimate, yes, with one translation: in a block wall the verticals sit in grouted cores (typically at 400–800 mm centres, matching the block module) and the horizontals sit in bond-beam courses, not at arbitrary spacings. Enter the core spacing as the vertical spacing and the bond-beam spacing as the horizontal spacing, and read the result as the bar schedule for the grouted cores and bond beams — then check it against the block manufacturer's coursing, since a 200 mm spacing that suits a concrete wall rarely matches a 400 mm block module.

Does the calculator include waste, dowels, tie wire or cost?

No — it gives the neat one-grid, one-wall total only. Add 5–10% waste for cutting and laps (one published grid guide recommends 10% for overlaps, cutoffs and waste), take footing dowels and starter bars from the footing calculator, estimate the fixing wire on the tie wire calculator, price the order with the cost calculator, and check the steel tonnage with the tonnage calculator. For a full takeoff across members use the quantity & takeoff calculator.
