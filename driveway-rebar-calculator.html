How to use: Driveway Rebar Calculator

  1. Enter driveway length and width in metres.
  2. Set the bar spacing and edge cover.
  3. Pick the bar size for the total.
  4. Read the bar count both ways, total length, weight and tie count.
  5. Add laps and waste before ordering — the calculator gives the neat mat, not the order quantity.

Formula

Bars each way = floor((dimension − 2 × cover) ÷ spacing) + 1, × kg/m for the weight.

See the full rebar weight chart (kg/m & lb/ft) →

Why subtract cover twice? The bars stop one cover short of each edge, so the span they cover is the dimension minus two covers. Why +1? A row of bars at both ends plus one per spacing interval — without the +1 you would be one bar short in each direction. What is a tie? Each crossing of a lengthwise and a crosswise bar is one tie-wire intersection; the calculator counts them so you can size tie wire with the tie-wire calculator.

Worked example 1: 10 m × 3 m driveway

Driveway 10 m × 3 m, 400 mm spacing, 75 mm edge cover, 12.7 mm (#4) bars. These are the values the calculator opens with, so you can check the result immediately:

  1. Bars along the length: floor((3.00 − 0.15) ÷ 0.40) + 1 = 8 bars, each 9.85 m long.
  2. Bars across the width: floor((10.00 − 0.15) ÷ 0.40) + 1 = 25 bars, each 2.85 m long.
  3. Total length: 8 × 9.85 + 25 × 2.85 = 150.05 m.
  4. Weight: 150.05 × 0.994 = 149.15 kg (328.82 lb), with 8 × 25 = 200 ties.

Try it yourself: enter the same numbers in the calculator above and you will get the same result.

Worked example 2: 10 m × 6 m two-car driveway

Driveway 10 m × 6 m, same 400 mm spacing, 75 mm cover, 12.7 mm bars:

  1. Bars along the length: floor((6.00 − 0.15) ÷ 0.40) + 1 = 15 bars, each 9.85 m long.
  2. Bars across the width: 25 bars, each 5.85 m long.
  3. Total length: 15 × 9.85 + 25 × 5.85 = 294.00 m.
  4. Weight: 294.00 × 0.994 = 292.24 kg (644.28 lb), with 15 × 25 = 375 ties.

Doubling the width roughly doubles the steel — the mat grows in both directions, so area, not just length, drives the total.

Closer spacing adds bars in both directions, so the total grows fast — dropping from 400 mm to 300 mm adds roughly a third more steel. Match spacing to the load, not to habit.

Wheel-track spacing: tighten only where the wheels run

Wheels do not load a driveway evenly — they follow two narrow tracks, and a tyre near the free edge bends the slab roughly twice as hard as the same wheel at mid-panel. That is why the standard grid can stay at 400 mm while the wheel lines go tighter.

On long driveways where every vehicle follows the same path to the garage (past about 18 m, or serving a shop or RV pad), tighten the spacing under the wheel lines only — for example from 400 mm to 300 mm inside two strips about 900 mm wide, centred on each wheel path. That is a third more steel inside the strips, but on a 6 m wide two-car driveway the strips cover only about 30% of the area, so the whole-driveway increase is roughly 10%.

On a narrow single-car driveway the strips cover most of the width, so just use the tighter spacing throughout instead of cutting strips. And never tighten the whole grid to chase wheels on a short drive where cars wander across the full width — you would pay to densify panels nobody drives on.

Joints and dowels: where the concrete is allowed to crack

Concrete cracks wherever it is allowed to; the joint layout decides where. Three joint types, three different steel rules.

Joint typeSpacingSteel across it
Control (contraction)2–3× slab thickness: 100 mm → 2.4–3.7 m; 125 mm → 3.0–4.6 m; 150 mm → 3.7–5.5 m; saw-cut ¼ deep within 24 hNone — stop the grid about 50 mm short of each side so the joint can open
Expansion (isolation)Where the driveway meets the garage slab, house footing, or street curbSmooth dowels (see below)
LongitudinalOne centre line on any driveway wider than 3.7 mNone needed on grade — keyway, or tie the grid through

Control-joint spacing follows the 2–3× rule: slab thickness in inches, read in feet. A 125 mm (5 in) slab gets joints every 3.0–4.6 m; wider than 3.7 m (12 ft) and the slab also needs a longitudinal joint down the middle.

Across expansion joints — and only there — go smooth dowel bars: 16 mm (#5) smooth steel at 300 mm centres, 450 mm long (225 mm embedded each side), greased on one half so the slabs can still slide independently. Smooth, not deformed: deformed rebar locks the slabs together and drags the garage slab wherever the driveway settles. A 6 m wide driveway at an expansion joint needs 6.00 ÷ 0.30 + 1 = 21 dowels — 21 × 0.45 = 9.45 m of 16 mm bar, or 9.45 × 1.578 = 14.91 kg.

Two don'ts: never put deformed rebar through a control joint (the joint cannot open, so cracks migrate to panel middles), and never put dowels across ordinary control joints — those must stay free to open.

Thickened edges: the cheap upgrade that prevents edge cracks

The cheapest crack preventer on a driveway is not more steel — it is more concrete at the edge. Over the outer 300 mm, deepen the slab to 1.5–2× its thickness (a 125 mm slab runs about 190–250 mm at the edge), tapering back to grade, with the grid bars running continuous through the thickened section and one bar bent down into the deepened toe.

The price is concrete only: a 10 m × 3 m driveway has 2 × (10 + 3) = 26 m of perimeter, and deepening it 75 mm over a 300 mm width adds 26 × 0.30 × 0.075 = 0.585 m³ — about 0.59 m³ of extra concrete — aimed at the single highest-stress zone on the slab. Skip it only where the edge is not free: a side fully confined by an existing garage apron, curb, or sidewalk is already supported.

Frost heave and the sub-base question

In hard-freeze climates the sub-base is a structural layer, not backfill: 150–300 mm of compacted granular material (crushed stone or clean gravel) under the whole drive, a 1–2% surface slope so meltwater drains instead of feeding the frost line, and a geotextile separator between stone and heavy clay so the sub-base cannot punch into the soil. Near a heated garage, a 25–50 mm strip of rigid insulation under the first 600–1,200 mm stops the frozen/unfrozen differential hinging the panel where cars cross it.

Does rebar stop frost heave? No — heave pressures lift whole panels, and no grid anchors a slab against that. The movement defence is sub-base, drainage, and joints that absorb the travel; the steel's frost-season job is second-hand, holding cracked panels flush so the surface keeps draining instead of trapping water in the crack that feeds next winter's heave. None of this changes the steel quantity the calculator gives — it decides whether that steel survives the winter intact.

Estimating the cost

Cost = steel weight × price per kg, plus tie wire and chairs. Using worked example 1 with an example price of €1.20/kg for steel: 149.15 × €1.20 = €178.98 for the bars. Tie wire and chairs sit on top — the calculator gives you the 200-tie count to size the wire with the tie-wire calculator. Prices vary by region and supplier, so get current quotes before budgeting. For a full materials picture, add the concrete volume from the concrete estimator.

What the calculator does not include

  • Apron flare steel. The flared apron where the driveway meets the street is extra steel — this sizes the main rectangular slab only.
  • Sub-base volume. The frost section above gives typical sub-base depths, but the volume itself is separate from the steel mat.
  • Joint cutting and dowel labour. The joints section above gives spacings and the dowel spec; cutting the joints and placing the dowels are site work, not quantities the calculator outputs.
  • Laps and waste. The result is the neat mat. Add lap lengths and a cutting-waste allowance before ordering.
  • Tie wire and chairs. The calculator counts the ties (200 in example 1) — size the wire with the tie-wire calculator and the supports with the chair calculator.
  • Concrete volume and placing costs. Volume comes from the concrete estimator; formwork and labour are separate.

5 common mistakes

  1. Leaving steel on the ground. Bars and mesh sitting on the sub-base end up with no cover and no bond at the bottom of the slab. Chair the mat up to the designed cover before the pour.
  2. Confusing cover with spacing. Cover is the edge distance (75 mm here); spacing is the bar-to-bar distance (400 mm). Swapping them changes both the bar count and the durability.
  3. Ordering the neat quantity. Laps between bars and cutting waste sit on top of the neat mat — order extra or the last strip runs short.
  4. Wrong thickness for the load. A 100 mm car slab under an RV cracks. Match thickness — and steel — to the heaviest vehicle that will use the driveway.
  5. Running steel through control joints. Deformed bars across a control joint lock the panels together, so the joint cannot open and cracks migrate to panel middles. Stop the grid short of control joints, and save dowels for expansion joints only.

Last reviewed: October 2026. Bar weights use d² ÷ 162.28 (metric) and ASTM A615 sizes (imperial). Thickness and spacing ranges are rule-of-thumb guidance for early estimates — always verify against your structural drawings and design code.

Driveway Rebar Calculator — FAQ

What size rebar for a driveway?

#3–#4 (10–13 mm) at 300–450 mm centres is common for residential driveways. The calculator opens on 12.7 mm (#4) at 400 mm centres — a solid standard driveway choice; go larger or closer-spaced for heavier vehicles.

Do driveways need rebar?

Reinforcement helps control cracking; thickness and bar size depend on loading. An unreinforced driveway slab cracks more freely as it shrinks and as loads cross it — steel does not prevent every crack, but it holds the slab together and spreads loads.

How much rebar for a 10 m × 3 m driveway?

At 400 mm spacing, 75 mm cover and 12.7 mm bars: 8 + 25 bars, 150.05 m of bar, 149.15 kg (328.82 lb), 200 ties. Add laps and waste before ordering.

How thick should a driveway be?

Typically 100 mm (4 in) for cars, 125 mm (5 in) for SUVs and light trucks, and 150 mm (6 in) or more for heavy vehicles and RVs. Thickness is a design decision — confirm it against your drawings, since it drives both concrete volume and steel.

What spacing should driveway rebar be?

300–450 mm centres covers most residential driveways; 400 mm is the calculator default. Lighter loads can go to 450 mm, heavier loads should close up to 300 mm. On long drives where wheels follow one path, tighten to 300 mm under the wheel lines only (see the wheel-track spacing section above).

Rebar or mesh for a driveway?

A tied rebar mat (what this calculator sizes) gives you known bar sizes and spacings. Welded mesh is an alternative, but it must be chaired up to mid-slab — mesh left on the ground ends up at the bottom of the pour, where it does almost no good.

How many bars do I need to order?

Order the calculator's bar count plus laps and waste. For the 10 m × 3 m example that is 33 bars (8 + 25) cut from stock — or about 13 sticks of 12 m bar before waste (150.05 ÷ 12 = 12.5).

Does the calculator include the apron?

No — it sizes the main rectangular slab only. The flared apron at the street is extra steel, and its shape (two triangles) needs its own allowance.

How much does driveway rebar cost?

Multiply the steel weight by your local price per kg. At an example €1.20/kg, the 10 m × 3 m driveway's bars cost €178.98; tie wire and chairs are extra. Thickened edges add concrete, not steel — about 0.59 m³ extra on the same 10 m × 3 m drive. Get current supplier quotes before budgeting.

Can I use imperial units?

The calculator takes metres and millimetres, but the equivalents are direct: 12.7 mm is a #4 bar, 400 mm is about 16 in, 75 mm is 3 in, and kilograms convert at × 2.20462 to pounds. Convert first, then enter metric values.
