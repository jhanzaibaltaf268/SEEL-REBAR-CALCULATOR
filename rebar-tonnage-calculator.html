How to use: Rebar Tonnage Calculator

  1. Pick the bar size — metric (mm) or US (# size) — from the dropdown.
  2. Enter the length of one bar in metres and how many pieces.
  3. Read the total tonnage in metric tons, the total weight in kg, and the US short tons.
  4. For a long-ton (UK) figure, divide the kg total by 1016.05 — the panel shows metric and US tons only.
  5. Add waste, laps and hooks before ordering — the panel converts neat bar weight only.

Formula

Weight (kg) = kg/m × length × quantity. Metric ton = kg ÷ 1000.

The formula, explained

Tonnage is just total weight expressed in a large unit. The panel weighs the bars first, then divides:

Weight (kg) = kg/m × length × quantity; Metric ton = kg ÷ 1000; US short ton = kg ÷ 907.185

The kg/m comes from the same d² ÷ 162.28 formula as the weight page (16 mm: 256 ÷ 162.28 = 1.578 kg/m). The two ton divisors are the whole story of this page: 1000 kg for the metric tonne, 907.185 kg for the US short ton (2000 lb × 0.45359237). A third divisor exists — 1016.05 kg for the UK long ton (2240 lb) — but the panel does not output it; every long-ton figure on this page is derived arithmetic (kg ÷ 1016.05) and is labelled as such. Three display notes. First, the panel shows four rows: total tonnage (metric, primary), total weight (kg), US tons, and per bar (kg), with the note "1.578 kg/m × 12m × 500 = 9,468 kg" on the defaults. Second, totals at or above 1000 show one decimal with separators ("9,468 kg", "1,000.5 kg"), tonnages at or above 1 show two decimals ("9.47", "1.06"), and tonnages below 1 show three decimals ("0.990", "0.003") — quote the panel forms and your reader's check will match. Third, the bar dropdown reads "1.578|16" and the option value is exactly "1.578|16", so this page's defaults carry no bar-option trap (unlike the weight and quantity pages) — but the kg/m values are still d²/162.28 options, so a d²/162 table gives slightly different totals, as the bridges section shows.

Worked example 1: defaults (500 × 16 mm × 12 m)

Open the calculator as it loads: 16 mm bars, length 12 m, quantity 500 pieces.

Weight per bar = 1.578 × 12 = 18.94 kg. Total weight = 18.94 × 500 = 9,468 kg.

Total tonnage 9.47 metric tons, 10.44 US short tons — and, derived, 9.32 UK long tons and 20,873.3 lb. The panel shows all four rows: 9.47 metric ton, 9,468 kg, 10.44 ton, 18.94 kg per bar, with the note "1.578 kg/m × 12m × 500 = 9,468 kg". The point of the page is in that one line: the same 9,468 kg of steel is 9.47, 10.44, or 9.32 depending on which ton you mean — a spread of 1.12 tonnes between the smallest and largest reading of the same order. Any document that says "tons" without saying which one is ambiguous by up to 12%.

Worked example 2: small order, two bar lists

Set 12 mm bars, length 6 m, quantity 200 pieces.

Weight per bar = 0.887 × 6 = 5.32 kg. Total weight = 5.32 × 200 = 1,064.4 kg.

Total tonnage 1.06 metric tons, 1.17 US short tons. Now the equivalence that catches people out: 100 × 12 mm × 12 m gives exactly the same figures (1,064.4 kg, 1.06 t, 1.17 US) — halving the length and doubling the count leaves the tonnage unchanged, because tonnage only sees total metres × kg/m. The same holds at the defaults scale: 1000 × 16 mm × 6 m is 9,468 kg, identical to 500 × 16 mm × 12 m (only the per-bar row differs: 9.47 kg instead of 18.94 kg). Tonnage answers "how much steel", never "in what pieces" — the bar list still matters for cutting, laps and delivery, even when the tonnage does not move.

Worked example 3: imperial (#4 bars)

Set the bar dropdown to #4 (12.7 mm, 0.994 kg/m), length 6.096 m (20 ft), quantity 100 pieces.

Weight per bar = 0.994 × 6.096 = 6.06 kg. Total weight = 6.06 × 100 = 605.94 kg.

Total tonnage 0.606 metric tons, 0.668 US short tons. A heavier imperial run: 100 × #8 × 20 ft → per bar 24.22 kg, total 2,421.9 kg, 2.42 metric tons, 2.67 US tons. Two traps to avoid. First, a #4 bar (12.7 mm, 0.994 kg/m) is NOT a 12 mm bar (0.887 kg/m) — swapping them changes the tonnage by 12.1%. Second, the US-ton column here is in short tons (2000 lb), not metric tonnes: 605.94 kg is 0.606 metric tonnes but 0.668 short tons, and a US supplier quoting "0.67 tons" for this order means the short-ton figure, not a rounded metric one.

40 ft bars vs 12 m bars

Forty feet is 12.192 m, not 12 m — 0.192 m longer per bar, which is +1.6% on every bar weight and therefore on every tonnage. At panel precision, 100 × 12 mm bars:

This is also why bars-per-tonne figures disagree between sources: 12 mm needs 94 twelve-metre bars to reach a tonne but only 93 forty-foot bars (92 = 994.92 kg → 0.995 t; 93 = 1,005.7 kg → 1.01 t), and 16 mm needs 53 vs 52 (51 = 981.19 kg → 0.981 t; 52 = 1,000.4 kg → 1.00 t). Whenever a bars-per-tonne figure appears without a bar length, it is incomplete — ask for the length before using it. And when a supplier's bundle is 40 ft bars, do not price it from a 12 m table: the 1.6% gap is 17 kg per hundred 12 mm bars, and 170 kg per thousand.

What this calculator does not cover

  • Waste, laps, hooks, bends, cranks and development — the panel converts neat bar weight only. Work laps on the lap calculator and hooks on the hook calculator and add them to the neat weight first.
  • Multi-size schedules — one bar size per run; run each size separately and add the tonnages, as the area page does for mixed cages.
  • Cost — price the tonnage on the cost calculator (it prices the with-waste weight; see the bridges section).
  • UK long tons as a panel row — derived only (kg ÷ 1016.05); the panel outputs metric tonnes and US short tons.
  • Reverse conversion (tonnes → bars) — use the weight calculator in Reverse mode.
  • Bar counts from dimensions or spacing — use the member tools (slab, footing, mat, column, beam, wall, pool, driveway) or the quantity calculator for area-density estimates and the concrete estimator for kg/m³-ratio estimates.
  • Trucks, bundles and delivery logistics — the trucks section gives planning figures only; confirm payload, trailer length and bundle sizes with your supplier.
  • Stainless or alloy densities, mesh, and cut-length optimisation against stock bars.
  • Stirrups, chairs and tie wire as separate lines — see the stirrup calculator, the chair calculator and the tie wire calculator; their weights must be added to the bar tonnage by hand.
  • Spacing checks against code maxima — the spacing calculator works bar counts from a length and a target spacing.

5 common mistakes

  1. Quoting "tons" without saying which. A US supplier's "10 tons" is 9.07 metric tonnes; a UK supplier's "10 tons" (long) is 10.16 metric tonnes; a metric "10 tonnes" is 11.02 US tons. The same number means three different orders, 12% apart at the extremes. Write the ton down — metric, short, or long — on every quote, delivery note and drawing.
  2. Ordering the exact tonne count instead of the ceil. A bars-per-tonne table gives the count that REACHES a tonne, not the count that EQUALS it. Order 93 × 12 mm × 12 m and you hold 0.990 tonnes, not one tonne; order 135 × 10 mm and you hold 0.998 tonnes. The ceil exists for exactly this reason — and on 32 mm bars it costs 60.1 kg of overshoot, which is the price of not falling short.
  3. Trusting the small-order ton columns. Below 1 tonne the panel shows three decimals, so one 6 mm bar reads 0.003 in both ton columns although the exact values differ by ~10% (0.002664 vs 0.002937). The displays match; the steel does not. Check the kg row whenever the tonnage is under 1 tonne.
  4. Mixing the constants, or the bar lengths. A d²/162 table gives ~0.2% more than this panel's d²/162.28 options (one popular tool's 200 × 12 mm × 6 m example: 1,066 kg vs 1,064.4 kg here). A 40 ft table gives 1.6% more per bar than a 12 m table (10.81 vs 10.64 kg on 12 mm). Either mix-up moves a multi-tonne order by tens of kilos — check the constant and the length before assuming an arithmetic error.
  5. Trusting a worked example without re-running it. Published tonnage examples do not always reproduce: one US tool's driveway example claims ~1,120 lb where its own stated inputs give 1,068.8 lb (it only reproduces with an unstated ~5% waste factor), and one popular guide's imperial formula overstates #8 bar by 16× (42.75 lb/ft vs the true 2.670 lb/ft). Re-run every example in the panel with your own bar list before relying on it — including the ones on this page.

Rebar Tonnage Calculator — FAQ

How many bars in a ton of rebar?

Divide 1000 by (kg/m × bar length). For 12 mm × 12 m bars, about 94 bars per ton — exactly 94 bars weigh 1,000.5 kg (1.00 metric ton) on this panel, while 93 bars weigh 989.89 kg (0.990 t) and fall short. The full table below gives every size at 12 m stock: 8 mm 212 bars, 10 mm 136, 12 mm 94, 16 mm 53, 20 mm 34, 25 mm 22, 32 mm 14. Note the ceil: ordering the bars-per-tonne count always overshoots the tonne slightly (by 0.5 kg on 12 mm but 60.1 kg on 32 mm), because the last bar must be bought whole.

What is the difference between metric and US tons?

A metric ton is 1000 kg; a US (short) ton is 907.2 kg (the panel uses 907.185 kg exactly). The same steel therefore reads about 10% higher in US tons: the calculator defaults (500 × 16 mm × 12 m) are 9.47 metric tons but 10.44 US tons. There is also a third ton — the UK long ton, 1016.05 kg — which reads lower than both (9.32 long tons for the defaults). Always ask which ton a quote, a truck limit or a drawing means: 'tonne' always means metric, but 'ton' on its own could be any of the three.

What is a long ton, and does the panel show it?

A UK long ton (imperial ton) is 2240 lb = 1016.05 kg — the traditional British shipping ton, still used in some maritime and legacy contexts. The panel does not show it: it outputs metric tonnes and US short tons only. To get it, divide the kg total by 1016.05 (derived arithmetic, not a panel row): the defaults order of 9,468 kg is 9.32 long tons, and 10,000 kg is 9.84 long tons. If a supplier quotes long tons and you compare against metric tonnes without converting, you will under-order by about 1.6%.

Why do the metric and US ton columns read the same on small orders?

Because below 1 tonne the panel shows three decimals, and the two values round to the same display. One 6 mm × 12 m bar is 0.002664 metric tons and 0.002937 US tons — a 10% difference in reality — but both display as 0.003. One 8 mm bar shows 0.005 in both columns for the same reason. From 10 mm up the columns separate (0.007 vs 0.008). Never conclude two tonnages are equal because their displays match below 1 tonne — check the kg row, which never hides the difference.

How many trucks do I need for an order?

The panel has no logistics output, so work it from the kg total (derived arithmetic): divide by your truck's payload and round up. At a 20,000 kg planning payload, the defaults order (9,468 kg) is 0.47 truckloads — one truck; double it (18,936 kg) is 0.95 — still one truck, just; 2000 × 16 mm × 12 m (37,872 kg) is 1.89 — two trucks; 1000 × 25 mm × 12 m (46,212 kg) is 2.31 — three trucks. At a 25,000 kg payload the last two become two trucks each, so state your payload assumption when you quote truck counts. And length binds before weight: 12 m bars need a 40 ft (12.2 m) trailer or longer regardless of tonnage.

How do I convert a supplier's US-ton quote to metric tonnes?

Multiply US short tons by 0.907185: 10 US tons = 9.07 metric tonnes. In the other direction, multiply metric tonnes by 1.10231: 10 metric tonnes = 11.02 US tons. For long tons, multiply by 1.01605 (10 long tons = 10.16 metric tonnes) or divide metric tonnes by 1.01605. Convert every quote to the same ton before comparing prices — the cost calculator prices per kg, so converting to kg first (US tons × 907.185, long tons × 1016.05) removes the ambiguity entirely.

Does the tonnage include waste?

No — the panel converts neat bar weight only. Add waste before converting if you want the ordered tonnage: the defaults order at +5% waste is 9,941.4 kg, which is 9.94 metric tons and 10.96 US tons (derived arithmetic — enter the with-waste weight in the panel's quantity field, or scale the tonnage by 1.05). Laps and hooks are separate from waste: work them on the lap calculator and the hook calculator and add them to the neat weight first. Price the final tonnage on the cost calculator.

Can I convert tonnes back to bars?

Not on this panel — it runs bars → tonnage only. For the reverse direction, use the weight calculator in Reverse mode: 2000 kg of 12 mm bar at 12 m stock gives 188 bars (187 full + 10.79 m offcut). Or divide by hand and round UP: bars = tonnes × 1000 ÷ (kg/m × length). Rounding down is the error — 93 × 12 mm × 12 m is 0.990 tonnes, not one tonne, so a '93 bars per tonne' order arrives 10.11 kg short.

Why does another site's tonnage differ from this panel's?

Usually one of four things, in order of likelihood: (1) the ton definition — short tons read ~10% higher than metric tonnes for the same steel; (2) the constant — d²/162 tables give ~0.2% more than this panel's d²/162.28 options (one popular tool's 200 × 12 mm × 6 m example is 1,066 kg on d²/162, 1,064.4 kg here); (3) the bar length — 40 ft bars weigh 1.6% more per bar than 12 m bars, so the same count gives a different tonnage; (4) waste in or out — a with-waste tonnage is higher by exactly the waste percentage. Check all four before assuming an arithmetic error.

Can I convert a whole project with one run?

Only if it is one bar size at one length. The panel takes a single size, length and quantity, so a real project needs one run per line of the bar schedule, added together — exactly as the area page does for mixed cages. For the weights themselves, take each member off its own tool (slab, footing, mat, column, beam, wall, pool, driveway) or the quantity calculator for area-density estimates and the concrete estimator for kg/m³-ratio estimates, then convert each weight here and add the tonnages.
